## five things worth knowing

### 1. Nepal’s flood recovery bill is moving into the billions

Nepal’s National Planning Commission has begun a Post-Disaster Needs Assessment after the 26 August floods, with officials expecting final losses to exceed the initial NPR 400 billion estimate by around 30 per cent. The government is preparing for a donor conference and considering a claim to the Loss and Damage Fund, while acknowledging that concessional borrowing may still be necessary. ([Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2026/09/08/nepal-looks-for-grants-for-flood-recovery-but-loans-may-be-unavoidable?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This is a substantive development since yesterday’s edition. The disaster is moving from emergency response into a fiscal question, with an estimated US$4 billion to US$5 billion financing requirement and a government already wary of adding to public debt.

Why it matters: climate loss is becoming sovereign-debt exposure in real time.

### 2. Sri Lanka is putting livelihoods inside its social-protection reform

Sri Lanka’s cabinet has approved a US$100 million ADB-financed programme covering six districts from November 2026 to October 2031. Among its provisions, 30,000 selected Aswesuma households will receive LKR 200,000 in seed capital, alongside entrepreneurship support and market and financial linkages; the programme also includes care-worker regulation, elderly day-care centres and a national Social Protection Plan. ([EconomyNext](https://economynext.com/sri-lanka-to-roll-out-100mn-adb-funded-social-protection-program-283016/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The design is worth watching because it tries to connect income support with livelihoods and care services. Whether the seed-capital component produces durable income gains will depend heavily on beneficiary selection, local markets and the quality of accompanying support.

Why it matters: Sri Lanka is testing a broader conception of social protection during its post-crisis recovery.

### 3. India’s food-safety problem is becoming harder to dismiss as sporadic enforcement failure

Government data reviewed by Reuters show that roughly one in five food samples tested annually since 2020 has been classified as unsafe or substandard. Nearly 8,000 food licences have been revoked over the past three years, while regulators have intensified inspections and closures. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/raids-shutdowns-warning-labels-indias-unprecedented-food-safety-push-2026-09-08/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The denominator needs care. Regulatory samples are unlikely to constitute a representative probability sample of everything Indians eat, so the 20 per cent figure cannot be interpreted as the prevalence of unsafe food in the market. It does, however, indicate a substantial enforcement problem within the products and establishments reaching regulators.

Why it matters: India needs better surveillance data alongside tougher enforcement if it wants to distinguish systemic risk from targeted detection.

### 4. India’s nutrition programme is getting more specific about what an Anganwadi meal should contain

The ninth Rashtriya Poshan Maah begins today with dietary diversity and adequate protein among its priorities. More consequential than the campaign itself are revised Supplementary Nutrition Programme guidelines that formally define hot cooked meals, require menu boards and introduce Anganwadi Management Committees. ([Akashvani News](https://newsonair.gov.in/9th-rashtriya-poshan-maah-to-be-launched-in-varanasi-on-wednesday/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

These are delivery rules rather than evidence of better nutrition. Their value will depend on food procurement, menu adherence, meal quality and consumption by children.

Why it matters: the policy is shifting some attention from enrolment and activity counts toward the content and accountability of nutrition services.

### 5. Bangladesh reports a 77.9 per cent literacy rate, but the measure tells us less than it appears to

Bangladesh’s Bureau of Non-Formal Education marked International Literacy Day on 8 September with a government estimate that 77.9 per cent of people aged seven and above are literate. ([BSS](https://www.bssnews.net/news/national/422378?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The statistic is useful as an access indicator, with a familiar limitation. Literacy classifications do not establish functional reading proficiency, comprehension or learning quality. South Asia’s education problem increasingly lies in the distance between schooling, nominal literacy and acquired skills.

Why it matters: education monitoring needs measures of what people can actually do with reading and numeracy, alongside participation indicators.

## one paper worth reading

### A useful new paper is harder to find today

Rather than promote an old working paper because it has been newly indexed, I would leave this section deliberately sparse.

One apparently new September listing that surfaced in the search, Sampreet Singh Goraya’s *How does Caste Affect Entrepreneurship? Birth vs Worth*, illustrates the problem. The Barcelona School of Economics page currently labels the working paper August 2026, but the downloadable paper itself is dated July 2019. Its empirical analysis uses the 2005 Economic Census and the 2001 and 2006 MSME censuses. ([BSE](https://bse.eu/research/working-papers/how-does-caste-affect-entrepreneurship-birth-vs-worth))

The paper remains interesting. It estimates that Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribe entrepreneurs owned 13 per cent of non-agricultural establishments despite representing 24 per cent of the population, and uses a calibrated entrepreneurship model to estimate output losses from caste-specific entry and financing constraints.

It is not new research. Treating repository dates or refreshed publication pages as publication dates would quietly degrade the purpose of this section.

I would rather publish no paper today than manufacture novelty.

## one number

### 20%

Approximately one in five food samples tested annually by Indian regulators since 2020 has been found unsafe or substandard, according to government data reviewed by Reuters. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/raids-shutdowns-warning-labels-indias-unprecedented-food-safety-push-2026-09-08/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There is an important sampling qualification. Food-safety testing is partly risk-directed, so this is not an estimate that 20 per cent of food sold in India is unsafe. The statistic describes the yield among samples regulators actually tested.

That distinction is exactly why India would benefit from publishing clearer sampling frames and separating surveillance sampling from complaint-driven and enforcement testing.

## policy watch

### Sri Lanka approves a US$100 million social-protection and livelihoods programme

Sri Lanka’s cabinet has approved implementation of the Macro-Recovery Sector Development Program for Promoting Resilience and Inclusive Economic Opportunities, financed through a US$100 million concessional ADB loan. It will operate in Kegalle, Matale, Mullaitivu, Kilinochchi, Batticaloa and Nuwara Eliya between November 2026 and October 2031. ([EconomyNext](https://economynext.com/sri-lanka-to-roll-out-100mn-adb-funded-social-protection-program-283016/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The most interesting design choice is the proposed LKR 200,000 seed-capital grant for 30,000 selected Aswesuma households. Cash transfers and enterprise support solve different problems. Combining them can make sense when households face capital constraints, although enterprise grants have uneven returns and cannot substitute for adequate consumption protection.

The programme therefore creates a useful evaluation opportunity. Household income, enterprise survival, consumption stability and graduation from Aswesuma should be tracked separately. A fall in benefit receipt by itself would tell us very little about whether livelihoods improved.

## editorial note

### The denominator problem

Two numbers in today’s edition illustrate a recurring problem in development statistics.

India’s 20 per cent food-sample failure rate sounds like a prevalence estimate. It is not, unless the tested samples represent the food supply. Bangladesh’s 77.9 per cent literacy rate sounds like a measure of learning. It is not, unless the classification captures meaningful reading ability.

Neither statistic is useless. Both become misleading when the denominator or construct disappears in retelling.

This matters because administrative systems are producing vastly more numbers than they once did. Publication has become easier. Interpretation has not.

A useful discipline for reading development data is therefore remarkably old-fashioned: ask who entered the denominator, how they got there, what exactly was measured, and what population the resulting estimate can describe. Many apparently large findings shrink considerably once those four questions are answered.
