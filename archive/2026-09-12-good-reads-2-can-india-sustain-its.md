Writing in the September issue of Finance & Development, the former RBI governor asks whether India can sustain its economic rise when employment, productivity and innovation remain persistent constraints. His timing is good. Much commentary on India continues to oscillate between celebration of its growth rates and predictions that it will become the world’s third-largest economy. Subbarao is interested in the machinery underneath those claims. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/can-india-sustain-its-rise-duvvuri-subbarao?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

One reason I found the essay worth reading is that it separates economic size from development performance. A country can climb global GDP rankings because of its population and aggregate growth while continuing to struggle with the quality of employment, productivity and the distribution of economic opportunity. Those questions matter more for understanding what growth is doing to people’s lives.

Subbarao also places India’s present difficulties within a changed external environment. Energy dependence, geopolitical instability and the limits of the China+1 opportunity complicate the assumption that India can simply reproduce the development trajectory followed by earlier Asian economies. The harder domestic problem is productivity. India needs workers to move into activities that produce considerably more value, while creating enough decent employment for a very large working-age population. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/can-india-sustain-its-rise-duvvuri-subbarao?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

I would push the argument further on labour. Recent evidence on falling real rural wages makes it difficult to treat employment generation as sufficient evidence of economic progress. The composition of employment, earnings and security matter. So does the large amount of women’s work that remains poorly paid, unpaid or statistically difficult to interpret.

The essay is useful precisely because it resists the inevitability embedded in much discussion of India’s rise. Becoming a larger economy is highly probable. Becoming a richer society with productive work available across a much broader section of the population is a different proposition.

***That distinction deserves considerably more attention.***
