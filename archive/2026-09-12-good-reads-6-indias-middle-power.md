*Good Reads 5# on Multilateralism*

There is a familiar way of describing the current international order: fragmentation, weakening multilateral institutions, trade conflict, industrial policy, strategic competition and a retreat from the assumptions that governed globalisation for several decades.

N. K. Singh begins from much the same diagnosis. But “The Middle Power Moment,” published in the June 2026 issue of Finance & Development, is less interested in mourning the old order than in asking who gains room to act as it weakens. His answer is the world’s middle powers, with India occupying a particularly interesting position. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/06/point-of-view-the-middle-power-moment-n-k-singh?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Singh’s central claim is straightforward. Influence in the emerging international system will depend less exclusively on economic or military weight and more on the capacity to convene countries, bridge competing blocs and build coalitions around particular problems.

That produces a world of smaller arrangements rather than one universal architecture. Climate finance might require one coalition, critical minerals another, migration another, energy security another. These groupings may be temporary and overlapping. Countries need not agree on everything in order to cooperate on something. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/06/point-of-view-the-middle-power-moment-n-k-singh?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For India, Singh sees an opportunity.

Its size matters, of course. So does its position across several political and economic groupings, its long diplomatic tradition of nonalignment, its relationships with developing economies and its ability to engage countries that increasingly find themselves on opposing sides of geopolitical disputes.

This is one of the essay’s strongest observations. The return of geopolitics does not necessarily recreate the clean blocs of the Cold War. Economic relationships now cut across political ones. Countries may compete on technology, cooperate on climate, depend upon one another for energy and disagree over security.

That makes the space between blocs politically valuable.

Singh argues that India should occupy it deliberately.

### economics has become foreign policy

What makes the essay particularly useful for this series is that Singh does not treat foreign policy as something separate from development economics.

Energy security, industrial capacity, migration, climate finance, technology and skills all appear in the essay as both domestic economic questions and instruments of international power.

Consider energy. India remains highly exposed to external oil markets while attempting a large transition towards non-fossil energy. Singh places that transition inside a wider problem of climate finance. Developing economies, he notes, require at least $310 billion each year for adaptation, while current flows are about $26 billion. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/06/point-of-view-the-middle-power-moment-n-k-singh?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Or consider industrial policy. Excess capacity in steel and solar manufacturing is no longer merely a question of prices and efficiency. It now produces tariffs, subsidies and political responses as governments try to protect domestic production while retaining access to global markets. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/06/point-of-view-the-middle-power-moment-n-k-singh?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Migration is treated similarly. Ageing societies in Europe and East Asia face shrinking workforces while India and much of Africa remain young. Singh argues that migration should be understood as a response to these demographic imbalances rather than primarily as a crisis. But he adds an obvious qualification: a young population becomes an economic advantage only when workers have the skills and employment opportunities to make use of it. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/06/point-of-view-the-middle-power-moment-n-k-singh?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Then there is technology. Singh points to the gap between India’s demand for AI professionals and the very small proportion of young people receiving formal skills training. His answer is sustained investment in human capital and research, alongside policies intended to spread productivity gains rather than deepen digital inequality. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/06/point-of-view-the-middle-power-moment-n-k-singh?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Read together, these are not four separate policy problems.

They amount to an argument that domestic economic capacity increasingly determines foreign-policy capacity.

### where I hesitate

I am persuaded by Singh’s description of the opportunity more readily than by the implied measure of Indian power.

“Middle power” is an unusually elastic category. India is simultaneously a very large economy, a nuclear-armed state, the world’s most populous country, a major military power, a lower-middle-income country and a country with substantial deficits in employment, education, health and state capacity.

Those facts coexist.

That makes aggregate size a poor guide to the kind of influence India can exercise.

A country may possess enough diplomatic weight to convene international coalitions while still facing severe domestic constraints on its ability to provide technology, finance, public goods or market access to partners. The distinction between being present at the table and being able to alter what happens there matters.

There is also a tension in the essay between plurilateralism as a practical response to institutional paralysis and plurilateralism as a substitute for universal institutions.

Smaller coalitions can act faster. They can also leave smaller and poorer countries with less influence. A world organised through shifting groups of powerful states may be more flexible than the old multilateral system without necessarily being fairer.

This is especially relevant for the Global South. India may gain bargaining space from fragmentation. Many much smaller developing economies may experience the same fragmentation primarily as vulnerability.

The question, then, is not simply whether India can become an effective middle power.

It is what India would use that position for.

### the India question

This is where Singh’s revival of nonalignment becomes interesting.

He is not proposing a return to the twentieth-century version of the Non-Aligned Movement. His argument is closer to strategic autonomy adapted to economic interdependence: cooperate across blocs, avoid unnecessary alignment and build issue-based coalitions where interests coincide. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/06/point-of-view-the-middle-power-moment-n-k-singh?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

India has already practised versions of this.

But the next stage will require more than diplomatic flexibility. Leadership among developing economies will depend partly on whether India can offer things those countries need: finance, technology, market access, medicines, digital public infrastructure, climate partnerships, knowledge and credible institutions for cooperation.

That moves the discussion back from diplomacy to development.

India’s external influence will ultimately rest not just on the cleverness of its positioning but on the economic and institutional capacity beneath it.

### why read it?

Because Singh gives us a compact statement of an argument likely to become increasingly common in Indian policy thinking: fragmentation in the global order is not merely a threat to manage but a source of political room for countries that can operate between competing centres of power.

The essay is most interesting when read alongside a harder question.

A middle-power moment may exist.

What, materially, would India need to do with it?
