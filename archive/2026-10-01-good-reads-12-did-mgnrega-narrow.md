*GoodReads #12 on 30th September 2026*

There is a tempting story about MGNREGA and women’s wages.

The programme offers rural households a statutory employment guarantee. Men and women are entitled to the same wage for the same work. In a labour market where women frequently have fewer employment options and weaker bargaining power, an alternative source of paid work should matter beyond the programme itself.

If an agricultural employer knows that a woman can instead take MGNREGA work at a specified wage, her reservation wage may rise. Employers may have to pay more to retain workers. Public employment can therefore influence private agricultural wages even for workers who are not currently on an MGNREGA worksite.

This is the possibility examined in a new open-access paper by Padma Lakshmi Govindarajan and K. Thomas Felix, published in *Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems* on 29 September 2026.

The paper is interesting because the answer it produces is less tidy than the theory.

## Wages rose. The gender gap largely remained.

The authors use Labour Bureau data from *Wage Rates in Rural India*, covering 20 states between 1995–96 and 2023–24. They examine wages across 11 agricultural occupations and compare patterns before and after the introduction of MGNREGA.

There is a gender wage gap.

At the aggregate level, the authors estimate that male agricultural workers earned about ₹5.01 more per day than women in the wage series they analyse. But averages hide considerable variation by task.

Statistically significant gaps appear in ploughing and tilling, harvesting, threshing, irrigation and general agricultural labour, cane crushing and animal husbandry. The estimated gaps range from ₹3.73 in threshing to ₹7.78 in ploughing and tilling.

In sowing, weeding, transplantation, picking and winnowing, by contrast, the estimated gender differences are not statistically significant.

Already, this complicates the idea of a single agricultural gender wage gap.

There are several.

They are associated with the work being performed, how that work is gendered, and the labour markets surrounding particular agricultural tasks.

## Then comes MGNREGA

The authors find higher agricultural wages in the post-MGNREGA period across the activities they study.

Their aggregate estimate is an increase of ₹15.43 in agricultural wages. Activity-specific estimates range from ₹7.50 per day for winnowing to ₹15.87 for sowing.

But here is the result that interested me.

The gender-by-post-MGNREGA interaction is statistically significant for only three activities: ploughing and tilling, harvesting and winnowing. For most agricultural tasks, the authors do not find statistically significant evidence that women’s wage trajectory relative to men’s changed after MGNREGA.

At the aggregate level, the interaction is also statistically insignificant.

In other words, the data are consistent with a period in which agricultural wages increased while gender wage inequality persisted.

Those are two different outcomes.

## A rising floor is not the same thing as a closing gap

This distinction is worth dwelling on.

Suppose men earn 100 and women earn 80.

A policy changes labour-market conditions and men subsequently earn 120 while women earn 100.

Women are earning more. The absolute gender difference is unchanged.

That is not a trivial improvement for the woman earning 20 more. But it is not wage equality either.

We often ask a single policy to carry both claims.

Did it improve women’s economic position?

Did it reduce gender inequality?

The answers need not be identical.

MGNREGA may give women access to paid work, establish an equal-wage rule within the programme and improve their bargaining position outside it. Yet private agricultural labour markets remain organised through occupational segregation, land relations, local labour demand, caste, gender norms, mobility and bargaining power.

A public employment programme can alter the outside option without rewriting all of those institutions.

The paper itself points towards these wider structural factors when discussing why wage differences persist across states and activities.

## But we need to be careful with the MGNREGA claim

This is also where the methods become important.

The paper uses an interaction-based panel regression comparing female and male wage patterns before and after MGNREGA. It resembles a difference-in-differences setup, but the authors explicitly say that it is not a conventional causal DiD design.

There is no untreated comparison group. MGNREGA was a national programme applying to rural workers of both sexes. The interaction therefore tells us whether gender wage patterns differed in the post-MGNREGA period, not what wages would have been in a counterfactual India without MGNREGA.

That distinction becomes even more important when the authors run a placebo test.

They assign 2000, several years before MGNREGA, as a false intervention date. The gender interaction is statistically significant even there.

That tells us gender wage trends were already changing before 2005. The pre-policy trajectories were not completely stable. The authors therefore caution against interpreting their post-2005 coefficients as causal effects of MGNREGA.

I think this is one of the paper’s most useful features.

A weaker reading would be: MGNREGA increased agricultural wages but failed to eliminate the gender wage gap.

The evidence does not quite permit that sentence.

What the study can establish more securely is that agricultural wages were higher in the post-MGNREGA period, gender wage differences persisted, and there is little consistent evidence in these data that the gender gap changed differently after MGNREGA.

That is less dramatic. It is also more defensible.

## There is another measurement problem here

The wage data themselves deserve attention.

The analysis constructs a long series from official Labour Bureau data covering nearly three decades. Occupational classifications changed during that period. Some activities had to be excluded because comparable female wage data were unavailable or because the series began only in 2014–15.

The resulting dataset is also effectively a pseudo-panel. It does not follow the same agricultural workers through time. Changes can therefore reflect both changes in wages and changes in the composition of workers represented in successive observations. The authors acknowledge this explicitly.

There are modelling difficulties too. Standard Hausman tests used to choose between fixed- and random-effects specifications did not produce reliable results for several activities. The authors ultimately use random-effects models with clustered standard errors, while setting out the assumptions this requires.

None of these issues makes the exercise pointless.

They change the question we should ask of it.

This is better read as a study of long-run wage patterns than as an estimate of the treatment effect of MGNREGA.

## The occupation matters

For me, the most interesting result may actually be the variation between agricultural activities.

We routinely discuss “women agricultural workers” as though this describes a homogeneous labour market.

It does not.

Women and men are differently distributed across tasks. Some agricultural work is culturally coded as male or female. Tasks differ in mechanisation, physical demands, skill classifications, seasonality and bargaining arrangements.

The study finds statistically significant gender wage differences in some activities and no statistically significant difference in others.

That should make us suspicious of a single average gender wage-gap number.

An aggregate tells us that inequality exists.

Disaggregation begins to tell us how it is produced.

And that matters for policy. If wage differences are partly generated through occupational segregation, an equal-wage provision alone cannot solve the problem. The question becomes who can enter which jobs, whose work is classified as skilled, who controls land and productive assets, who can travel for employment, who has competing employment options and how wages are actually negotiated.

## There is a useful lesson here about women’s economic empowerment

Women’s economic empowerment is sometimes treated as a sequence:

**employment → income → bargaining power → autonomy**

But each arrow contains an empirical question.

Paid employment does not guarantee equal wages.

Higher wages do not necessarily mean the gender wage gap has narrowed.

Equal statutory wages do not guarantee equal wages throughout the surrounding labour market.

And greater earnings do not automatically tell us who controls those earnings inside a household.

This paper only examines one part of that chain. That is precisely why I find it useful.

It does not need to establish that MGNREGA caused every change in rural women’s economic position. It shows us something more specific: a major public employment programme can coexist with persistent gender differentiation in the private agricultural labour market.

## What I take from the paper

The finding I would carry forward is not that MGNREGA “worked” or “didn’t work” for gender equality.

The evidence here cannot sustain either verdict.

Instead, the paper gives us a reason to separate three questions that are too often collapsed:

Did rural wages rise?

Did women’s wages rise?

Did the distance between women’s and men’s wages narrow?

Those are different tests.

A programme may do well on the first two while producing much less movement on the third.

That distinction matters far beyond MGNREGA. It applies whenever we use improvements in women’s absolute economic position as evidence that gender inequality itself has diminished.

Sometimes everybody moves forward while the distance between them remains.

That distance is worth measuring too.
