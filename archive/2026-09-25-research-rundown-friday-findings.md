Heat mortality before the heat arrives, the limits of digital inclusion, Bangladesh’s missing MSME credit, and an Asian economy caught between energy prices and El Niño.

This was a thinner research week than the previous one. I found six pieces worth carrying. The most interesting material concerns measurement and anticipation: whether we can estimate mortality before a climate shock occurs, whether digital access tells us anything useful about digital independence, and whether formal financial inclusion statistics capture firms that abandon the credit process before applying.

### 1. India could face 15,800 additional heat deaths from the current El Niño

Greenstone, Michael, Tamma Carleton, Genevieve Maricle, and Emily Grover-Kopec. 2026. *451,000 at Risk: Where Action Can Save Lives During the Super El Niño*. Climate Impact Lab, September 2026.

This is the paper I would read first, with one large qualification.

Climate Impact Lab estimates that the current El Niño could produce 451,000 additional heat-related deaths globally between June 2026 and February 2027 relative to temperatures in an average year. For the six months from September through February, the estimate is 239,000 ± 11,000. India accounts for an estimated 15,800 additional deaths over that six-month period, including about 7,400 between December and February. [Climate & Sustainable Growth Institute](https://climate.uchicago.edu/news/more-than-451000-heat-deaths-projected-from-super-el-nino/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That distinction between periods matters because several reports of the study have incorrectly described 451,000 as a six-month estimate. It covers June through February. The six-month forward estimate is 239,000. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2026/9/23/scientists-warn-super-el-nino-could-cause-450000-deaths-should-we-worry?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The modelling combines estimated temperature-mortality relationships across 24,378 regions with seasonal temperature forecasts, allowing local climate and vulnerability to alter the mortality response. The counterfactual is the corresponding months under average 1996–2025 conditions. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2026/9/23/scientists-warn-super-el-nino-could-cause-450000-deaths-should-we-worry?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

There is real analytical value here. Heat policy usually responds to forecasts of temperature. This work attempts to translate those forecasts into expected mortality at geographically useful scales.

There is also substantial uncertainty. These are modelled deaths, rather than observed mortality. Seasonal temperature forecasts carry error, the temperature-mortality functions are estimated, and the method has yet to appear in the peer-reviewed paper the authors say is forthcoming. The India figure should therefore be treated as a forecast with uncertainty rather than an expected death count waiting to happen.

The timing is unusual enough to matter for Indian heat planning. Nearly half of the projected September-February mortality occurs during December-February, outside the months in which Indian heat preparedness is usually concentrated. [Climate & Sustainable Growth Institute](https://climate.uchicago.edu/news/more-than-451000-heat-deaths-projected-from-super-el-nino/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This deserves a longer Research Rundown treatment if the underlying methodological paper appears.

### 2. ADB puts numbers on the joint energy and El Niño shock

Asian Development Bank. 2026. *Asian Development Outlook September 2026: Navigating Prolonged Energy Shocks and El Niño*. Manila: Asian Development Bank. DOI: 10.22617/FLS260387-3.

ADB now expects developing Asia and the Pacific to grow by 5.0% in 2026 and 5.1% in 2027. South Asia is forecast at 6.4% and 6.5%, respectively. India is projected to grow by 7.0% in FY2026, with consumption, investment and services exports partly offsetting higher energy costs and a weaker monsoon. [Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/publications/asian-development-outlook-september-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The useful part is the scenario analysis. ADB models the macroeconomic consequences of a very strong El Niño alongside prolonged disruption in energy markets. Across the region, energy costs and food-supply effects create the familiar combination of slower output growth and higher inflation. [Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/publications/asian-development-outlook-september-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Nepal shows why regional averages conceal a great deal. Following the August floods, ADB cut its FY2027 growth forecast from 4.5% to 4.1% and raised its inflation forecast to 5.3%. Damage to hydropower, roads and other productive assets is expected to depress activity while reconstruction raises expenditure and demand for materials. [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/money/2026/09/23/bhotekoshi-floods-prompt-adb-to-cut-nepal-s-growth-forecast-to-4-1-percent?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The numbers are forecasts, and they should be read as such. Their value lies in the explicit assumptions and scenario comparisons rather than the second decimal place of any growth estimate.

There is a direct thread here from the recent Daily on South Asia’s simultaneous energy and El Niño exposure. The full ADO supplies the modelling behind that story.

### 3. India’s digital divide becomes much steeper with age

Paul, Sohini, and Sudeshna Dey. 2026. “Making India’s Digital Public Infrastructure Work for Older Citizens.” *Ideas for India*, 25 September 2026. Based on Dey and Paul, “Bridging India’s Generational Digital Divide: Rethinking Digital Public Infrastructure for Elderly Inclusion,” *CSI Transactions on ICT*.

Paul and Dey use the nationally representative NSS Comprehensive Modular Survey on Telecom to separate digital access from capability and safety among older Indians.

The age gradient is large. Smartphone access falls from 88.6% among adults aged 18–49 to 44.7% among those aged 60–69 and 42% among those aged 70 and above. Among people with smartphone access, recent use remains above 97% across age groups. Access to an appropriate device therefore appears to be part of the constraint. [Ideas for India](https://www.ideasforindia.in/topics/governance/making-indias-digital-public-infrastructure-work-for-older-citizens)

Capability falls further down the chain. About 53.1% of adults aged 60 and above report being able to undertake online banking, against 71.4% among 18–49-year-olds. Only 8% of people aged 60 and above report knowing how to complain about cybercrime through the official portal. The figure is 6% among older women and 4% among older rural residents. [Ideas for India](https://www.ideasforindia.in/topics/governance/making-indias-digital-public-infrastructure-work-for-older-citizens)

The authors also estimate regressions controlling for gender, social group, religion, rural or urban residence, household internet access, consumption expenditure and state. Adults aged 70 and above have about 65% lower adjusted odds of being able to use the internet than adults aged 50–59, and 52% lower adjusted odds of conducting online banking. [Ideas for India](https://www.ideasforindia.in/topics/governance/making-indias-digital-public-infrastructure-work-for-older-citizens)

These remain observational associations. Age captures accumulated differences in schooling, employment, exposure to technology and other characteristics that controls will only partly absorb. Self-reported ability also differs from demonstrated ability.

The measurement point is stronger. Counting enrolments, IDs, accounts or transactions can substantially overstate effective access to a digital public service. Independence, successful task completion, assistance, failed transactions and grievance resolution belong in the outcome set.

That connects directly to last week’s financial-health piece. Both papers expose the same problem: counting an input as though it were the outcome.

### 4. Bangladesh’s MSMEs have digital footprints. Very few receive digital credit

LightCastle Partners. 2026. *Ecosystem Mapping and Diagnostic Study on MSME Financing in Bangladesh*. Research conducted under The Asia Foundation’s Bridging the Gap programme; findings disseminated 22 September 2026.

This is a useful study with a modest sample.

LightCastle surveyed 329 MSMEs across Bangladesh’s eight administrative divisions and supplemented the survey with 19 key-informant interviews, 12 in-depth interviews and four focus groups. It estimates a national MSME financing gap of US$2.8 billion. [Daily Sun](https://www.daily-sun.com/business/899232?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The descriptive results reveal a striking break in the credit pathway. Some 87% of surveyed firms had a digital footprint and 97% used mobile financial services, yet only 11.55% had accessed digitally enabled credit. Although 86% reported having borrowed, only 58% had formally applied for loans. [Daily Sun](https://www.daily-sun.com/business/899232?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The gender result deserves attention. Women-led firms used more digital platforms and showed strong interest in digital credit, while women-led businesses continue to receive a small share of formal SME lending. The report argues that collateral requirements and documentation remain important barriers. [The Daily Star](https://online.thedailystar.net/business/news/women-led-msmes-lead-digital-finance-still-face-heavy-loan-barriers-study-4280846?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

I would be careful with the national financing-gap estimate. A survey of 329 enterprises can describe the sampled firms well if the sampling design is sound. Scaling from that sample to a dollar estimate for a sector containing roughly 7.16 million firms requires assumptions that need inspection in the full technical report. The dissemination material available online gives much more information about findings than about weighting and extrapolation.

The more interesting result may be the application gap. Credit exclusion begins before a lender rejects an application. Firms that expect rejection, cannot satisfy documentation requirements or regard available products as unsuitable disappear from approval-rate statistics.

### 5. Indian cities have more money coming. The institutional problem remains

Ara, Shamim, and Aditi Pathak. 2026. “Promoting Ease of Living in Indian Cities.” *Ideas for India*, 21 September 2026.

This is substantive policy analysis rather than a new empirical paper, and I am including it because it follows neatly from last week’s WRI work on transitional urban areas.

Ara and Pathak examine municipal finance and service delivery as India’s urban population becomes increasingly concentrated in large urban agglomerations. They draw attention to the Sixteenth Finance Commission’s recommended ₹3.56 lakh crore allocation to urban local bodies for 2026–31, compared with ₹1.21 lakh crore under the Fifteenth Finance Commission. Eighty per cent is structured as basic grants and 20% as performance grants. A ₹56,100 crore special infrastructure component targets wastewater management, while a ₹10,000 crore urbanisation premium addresses peri-urban integration. [Ideas for India](https://www.ideasforindia.in/topics/urbanisation/promoting-ease-of-living-in-indian-cities?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The piece is useful beside WRI’s transitional-urban-areas paper because money and administrative geography meet here. If settlements spill across municipal boundaries, assigning funds to individual urban local bodies cannot by itself solve metropolitan transport, wastewater, housing or land-use problems.

There is little causal evidence in the article. Claims about which cities provide a better quality of life rely partly on composite Ease of Living measures, which combine heterogeneous indicators and citizen perceptions. Rankings of this kind deserve the same scrutiny as any composite index.

The institutional question is still worth retaining: whether larger transfers can produce better services when metropolitan functions cross jurisdictions and municipalities have limited own-source revenue and planning authority.

### 6. Nepal’s flood is now showing up in the public accounts

Asian Development Bank. 2026. Nepal country assessment in *Asian Development Outlook September 2026*, supplemented by the Government of Nepal’s post-flood assessments.

Nepal is worth separating from the regional ADO because the August disaster has created an unusually clear link between climate shock, infrastructure loss and fiscal pressure.

ADB now projects FY2027 growth of 4.1%, down 0.4 percentage points from its July forecast. The fiscal deficit is projected at 4.7% of GDP, compared with a pre-disaster projection of 3.4%. Public debt had already reached about 44.9% of GDP in FY2026. [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/money/2026/09/23/bhotekoshi-floods-prompt-adb-to-cut-nepal-s-growth-forecast-to-4-1-percent?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The government’s preliminary reconstruction estimate is NPR 723.31 billion. More than 430 MW of electricity generation, roughly one-tenth of Nepal’s installed capacity, was forced off the grid after the floods. A separate Nepal Tourism Board assessment estimates NPR 52.67 billion in direct and induced tourism losses. [Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/money/2026/09/23/bhotekoshi-floods-prompt-adb-to-cut-nepal-s-growth-forecast-to-4-1-percent?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The tourism estimate needs particular care. Its induced-loss component uses a six-month projection based partly on tourism income over the previous two years. It is therefore an estimate of foregone activity under stated assumptions, rather than an observed loss. [The Diplomat Nepal](https://diplomatnepal.com/news/preliminary-assessment-shows-tourism-sector-suffered-rs-52-67-billion-losses-from-bhotekoshi-trishuli-floods/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is becoming an unusually useful case for studying post-disaster public finance. Reconstruction spending can raise measured GDP while households, infrastructure and public balance sheets remain worse off. GDP growth during reconstruction is a poor measure of welfare recovery.

I would keep Nepal in view for the flagship rather than treat each new damage estimate as a separate Daily.

## what I would keep

The Climate Impact Lab analysis is the week’s most consequential piece and the one requiring the most caution. A mortality forecast is much harder to communicate than an observed mortality estimate. The distinction becomes especially important when the headline number is already being reported with the wrong time period. [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/features/2026/9/23/scientists-warn-super-el-nino-could-cause-450000-deaths-should-we-worry?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The Paul-Dey analysis is the cleanest empirical contribution. Its larger lesson is familiar from financial inclusion, social protection and health systems: administrative reach is a poor proxy for effective use. The Bangladesh MSME study reaches a related conclusion from the firm side. Digital traces are widespread. Credit remains scarce.

I found no new open-access South Asian nutrition study from 19–25 September with enough empirical substance to include. The same applies to WEE research beyond the gender results embedded in the Bangladesh MSME study. Six pieces are enough.
