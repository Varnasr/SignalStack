Public Health Initiative’s *The Health Gap* begins with two people of the same age.

One reaches Friday expecting a weekend, some rest, and a return to work on Monday. Somewhere else, another person works through Saturday and Sunday, continues working when sick, and eventually dies earlier.

The contrast establishes the article’s argument quickly. Health is distributed unequally because the conditions in which people live are distributed unequally.

The piece draws on Michael Marmot’s work on health inequalities and the social determinants of health. It describes the health gap as the relationship between social and economic position, health and life expectancy, rather than treating health principally as a consequence of biology or access to medical care.

Then it makes a much stronger claim:

> “The health gap in reality is a wealth gap.”

That is where the piece becomes particularly interesting.

## The gradient matters

The basic insight is difficult to dispute.

Health is not distributed randomly across populations. Neither is illness. Income, occupation, education, housing, food, working conditions, pollution, sanitation, social protection and the ability to obtain care are all socially patterned.

The article makes this concrete through everyday differences between richer and poorer households. Someone with greater economic security may have better food, more time, opportunities for physical activity, access to mental-health care and the ability to take time away from work when ill. Someone without that security may have far fewer choices.

The important word here is not simply poverty.

It is gradient.

The Marmot tradition is important partly because it moved discussion beyond a binary division between “the poor” and everybody else. Health outcomes tend to vary across the social hierarchy. The question is therefore not only why the poorest experience worse health. It is why health changes systematically as social position changes.

That distinction matters for policy. A programme aimed only at people below a poverty threshold addresses something different from a social structure in which health varies across the distribution.

## But is wealth the determinant?

Here I would be more cautious than the article.

Saying that wealth strongly predicts health is not quite the same as saying that wealth determines health.

Income and wealth buy things that matter for health. They affect housing, food, neighbourhood, transport, education and access to care. They can provide protection against shocks. They influence whether a person can stop working when sick rather than choosing between illness and lost income.

But socioeconomic position is also entangled with other institutions and relationships.

Consider two workers earning similar amounts. One has a permanent job, paid sick leave, predictable hours and health insurance. The other works casually, loses income whenever she misses a day, has little control over her schedule and faces regular occupational hazards.

Their incomes may look similar. Their exposure to health risks does not.

Or consider two households with comparable incomes living in places with very different air quality, water systems, public health services or exposure to extreme heat.

Again, income alone does not describe the relevant conditions.

Gender, caste, race, migration status, disability, geography and political power can affect health independently and through their interaction with economic position.

Wealth matters enormously. But it is partly a resource and partly a marker for access to a much wider set of resources, protections and forms of control.

## Measurement makes this harder

There is also a measurement problem hiding inside the argument.

What exactly do we mean by socioeconomic status?

Income?

Consumption?

Assets?

Occupation?

Education?

Housing?

Wealth?

A composite index?

These measures are related, but they are not interchangeable.

This becomes particularly important in development research. Household consumption may tell us something that reported monthly income does not. An asset index can rank households without telling us whether they have enough liquid resources to survive an emergency. Household wealth tells us little about who within the household controls it.

Even apparently straightforward measures can therefore produce different versions of the health gradient.

And then causality runs in both directions.

Low socioeconomic status can damage health. Poor health can also reduce earnings, interrupt education, exhaust savings, alter employment and push households into poverty.

A correlation between wealth and health can contain both processes at once.

## The India question

This becomes especially interesting in India because socioeconomic position cannot be separated neatly from the institutions through which people encounter health risks.

A woman’s ability to seek treatment may depend on household income, but also on whether she controls money, can travel alone, has time away from unpaid work and is taken seriously when she reaches a health facility.

A construction worker’s health is related to earnings, but also to occupational exposure, employment arrangements, housing and access to social protection.

A child’s health reflects household resources, but also sanitation, air pollution, nutrition, maternal health, schooling and the quality of public services available where the family lives.

The analytical question is therefore not simply whether poorer people have worse health.

We know that inequality matters.

The more useful question is how economic inequality becomes biological inequality.

What are the pathways?

Which can public policy interrupt?

And at what point in the life course?

## Where I disagree with the piece

*The Health Gap* ends in deliberately stark terms. It describes health inequality as a world in which those with money occupy the position of the strong and those without it the weak, ultimately determining “who lives and dies.”

I understand the force of that formulation. I am less persuaded by its reduction of the problem to wealth.

If wealth alone were the principal mechanism, redistribution would do most of the work.

Redistribution matters. But so do labour law, universal health care, clean air, safe water, housing, nutrition, education, social protection, gender relations, working conditions and the quality of public institutions.

The distinction is important because diagnosis shapes prescription.

Calling the health gap a wealth gap directs our attention towards inequality in resources.

Calling it a social gradient in health asks a broader question: what is it about the organisation of society that allows social position to enter the body?

That question is harder.

I think it is also closer to the reason Marmot’s work remains so useful.

The most interesting thing about the health gap is not simply that money can buy better health.

It is that inequalities which appear social, economic and political eventually become measurable as differences in illness, disability and years of life.

Public health begins much earlier than the clinic.
