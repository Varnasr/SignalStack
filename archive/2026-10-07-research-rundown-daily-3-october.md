## FIVE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

### 1. The energy shock is getting considerably larger

The World Bank’s latest commodity data, released on 2 October, show its energy price index rising **25.7 per cent in September alone**. Crude oil increased 21.2 per cent and natural gas 15.3 per cent. Food prices rose 3.8 per cent. Fertiliser prices were comparatively stable, increasing 0.4 per cent.

[World Bank commodity price data](https://www.worldbank.org/en/research/commodity-markets?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

For South Asia, the energy number matters well beyond petrol and electricity. India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Nepal remain exposed to imported fuel costs to varying degrees. Higher energy prices can pass through to transport, fertiliser production, food distribution, industrial costs and government budgets.

The monthly change is striking, though it should not be mistaken for a forecast. Commodity prices can reverse quickly. The relevant question for South Asian economies is how long the shock persists and how much governments absorb through taxes, subsidies, administered prices or fiscal support.

### 2. Pakistan has adopted a national mental health policy centred on primary and community care

Pakistan launched its National Mental Health Policy on 1 October. The policy proposes moving much of mental-health provision closer to households through primary healthcare, schools and community services.

Health Minister Syed Mustafa Kamal said around **24 million people** in Pakistan require psychiatric assistance, while the country has fewer than one psychiatrist per 500,000 people.

[Dawn report on Pakistan’s National Mental Health Policy](https://www.dawn.com/news/2034059/govt-launches-policy-to-bring-mental-healthcare-closer-to-families?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The direction makes sense in a system with such severe specialist shortages. Implementation will be harder. Moving services into primary care requires trained workers, referral systems, medicines, supervision and financing. A policy that decentralises the location of care without supplying those inputs simply relocates scarcity.

The useful indicators now are therefore operational: how many primary-care facilities actually provide mental-health services, who delivers them, what happens after referral, and whether poorer and rural households use them.

### 3. Pakistan is putting Rs87 billion into coastal resilience in Sindh

The Sindh government has launched a **Rs87 billion coastal resilience project** covering Thatta, Sujawal and Badin, with a stated target of one million beneficiaries.

The project involves the provincial government, the International Fund for Agricultural Development, Asian Development Bank and Green Climate Fund.

[Dawn report on the Sindh Coastal Resilience Project](https://www.dawn.com/news/2034097/rs87bn-coastal-resilience-project-launched-for-three-sindh-districts?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The geography matters. These coastal districts face overlapping exposure to salinity, cyclones, flooding, declining agricultural productivity and livelihood insecurity.

The one-million-beneficiary figure deserves scrutiny as implementation begins. Climate programmes often report people “reached” without distinguishing between direct recipients, households living within a project area and people experiencing measurable reductions in risk. Those are different quantities.

This project will be worth following for its measurement system as much as its expenditure.

### 4. Sri Lanka has kept its inflation target at 5 per cent for another three years

Sri Lanka’s government and central bank have retained a **5 per cent inflation target** under the country’s Monetary Policy Framework Agreement.

The Central Bank of Sri Lanka said the decision followed a scheduled review covering the country’s economic structure, empirical evidence, monetary-policy considerations, stakeholder views and international experience.

[Central Bank of Sri Lanka announcement](https://www.cbsl.gov.lk/en/news/SLs-inflation-target-is-set-at-5-for-the-next-three-years-20261002?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is significant because Sri Lanka’s recent experience with inflation was extreme. The country is now moving from crisis stabilisation into the harder task of establishing a credible medium-term monetary framework.

A numerical target cannot establish credibility on its own. Fiscal policy, exchange-rate pressures, energy and food prices, and the central bank’s capacity to respond to shocks will determine how binding the target becomes in practice.

### 5. Nepal’s floods are disrupting maternity care weeks after the water receded

At least **33 pregnant women from Rasuwa and Nuwakot have travelled to Kathmandu to give birth** because health services and roads remain disrupted after the 26 August floods. Four women in labour had to be airlifted.

[Kathmandu Post report on maternity services after the floods](https://kathmandupost.com/health/2026/10/02/floods-drive-33-pregnant-women-to-kathmandu-for-childbirth?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is a useful example of why disaster mortality alone is an inadequate health indicator. Flood exposure can continue through interrupted antenatal care, inaccessible facilities, medicine shortages, damaged roads and delayed emergency referrals.

The denominator also matters. Thirty-three transfers tell us that disruption occurred. They do not tell us the proportion of pregnant women affected, how many missed antenatal visits, whether institutional delivery rates changed or whether maternal and neonatal outcomes worsened.

Those are the data needed to measure the health burden of the disaster rather than its immediate physical damage.

## ONE PAPER WORTH READING

### Does ageing matter for public health spending in South Asia?

Jalandhar Pradhan, Soumyanayani Mahali and Tapan Swain examine the relationship between population ageing and government health expenditure across South Asian countries in a new peer-reviewed paper in *BMC Health Services Research*, published on 1 October.

[Read the open-access paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12913-026-15545-4?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The authors use cross-country panel data and several econometric specifications, including Driscoll-Kraay standard errors, panel-corrected standard errors and three-stage least squares. The paper treats demographic change alongside physician availability, unemployment and other macro-level determinants of public health expenditure.

Its regional focus is useful. South Asia is ageing while several countries still carry large maternal, child-health and infectious-disease burdens. Public systems therefore face overlapping rather than sequential epidemiological demands.

The limitation is identification. This is an observational macro-panel analysis. Alternative estimators can address particular statistical problems, including cross-sectional dependence and endogeneity assumptions, but they do not turn the relationship between ageing and expenditure into a clean causal estimate. Country-level expenditure data also conceal large differences in who receives public spending and what governments purchase.

Read it for the fiscal question it raises rather than a single coefficient: how should health budgets change when population ageing begins before older health burdens have disappeared?

## POLICY WATCH

### SDG budgeting is moving into the machinery of state finance

NITI Aayog, the Government of West Bengal and UNDP held a national workshop on **SDG budgeting** in Kolkata on 2 October under the State Support Mission.

[NITI Aayog](https://www.niti.gov.in/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The idea is straightforward: connect government expenditure with measurable Sustainable Development Goal outcomes rather than treating SDG reporting as a separate planning exercise.

The difficult part is attribution. A budget line can be tagged to an SDG with relative ease. Establishing whether the expenditure changed an outcome requires programme data, plausible indicators, expenditure tracking and, where the question warrants it, evaluation.

This distinction matters because budget tagging can create an appearance of measurement without producing evidence about spending effectiveness. The next step should therefore be to link financial classifications to administrative and outcome data that allow states to ask what each rupee purchased.

## EDITORIAL NOTE

Today’s stories share a problem that public policy repeatedly encounters: **the headline indicator often arrives before the useful one**.

Pakistan can announce 24 million people needing mental-health support. Sindh can identify one million intended beneficiaries of a climate project. Nepal can count 33 pregnant women transferred after floods. Governments can classify expenditure against the SDGs.

Each number establishes something. None tells us whether a public system worked.

The harder measures come later: treatment received, risk reduced, care interrupted, outcomes changed, money converted into services.

That gap between administrative counting and outcome measurement is where a surprising amount of development policy disappears.

### A question for readers

For large climate-adaptation programmes such as Sindh’s new coastal project, what should count as a beneficiary: residence inside the project area, receipt of an intervention, demonstrated reduction in exposure, or a measurable improvement in household resilience?

If you know of adaptation programmes that have handled this denominator problem well, send them my way. It is a measurement question worth examining in Methods Monday.
