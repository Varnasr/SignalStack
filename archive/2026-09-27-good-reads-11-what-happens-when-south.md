*Good Reads 11# on 27th September 2026 *

For most of the past several decades, the education problem in South Asia has been framed around expansion.

There were more children. Governments needed more schools, more teachers and more classrooms. The policy task was to get children into school and keep them there.

That demographic assumption is beginning to change.

Mariana Marchionni, Emmanuel Jose Vazquez and Monica Yanez Pagans point to a striking projection in a recent World Bank essay: South Asia’s population of children under 15 is expected to fall from about 420 million in 2024 to 352 million in 2050. That is a decline of roughly 16 per cent. [World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/demographic-change-is-coming-to-south-asia-s-classrooms--will-ed?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Their argument is deceptively simple. If education systems have fewer children to educate, governments may be able to spend more on each child without spending a larger share of national income on education.

The underlying World Bank research goes further. Its expenditure modelling suggests that demographic change could make resources equivalent to about 0.6 percentage points of GDP available for reinvestment in basic education by 2050, on average, even after allowing for universal basic education. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-11303?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That sounds like unusually good news for a region with a persistent learning problem.

I think the more interesting question is what has to happen for the arithmetic to become an educational gain.

## Fewer children is not the same as cheaper education

The first problem is that education systems do not shrink neatly.

Suppose a school goes from 200 pupils to 160. Its roof still needs repairing. It may still need a head teacher, electricity, toilets and teachers across several grades. Transport costs do not disappear. Administrative systems remain.

A 20 per cent decline in pupils does not therefore produce a 20 per cent decline in costs.

The World Bank authors make precisely this point. Education contains substantial fixed costs. If governments leave the organisation of schooling unchanged while enrolment falls, expenditure per pupil can rise without releasing much money for improving learning. [World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/demographic-change-is-coming-to-south-asia-s-classrooms--will-ed?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

There is useful international experience here. The authors point to Europe and Central Asia, where declining student populations did not automatically generate efficiency gains. School structures often remained in place as enrolment fell. [World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/demographic-change-is-coming-to-south-asia-s-classrooms--will-ed?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This distinction matters because “fiscal space” can sound rather more tangible than it is.

Demography creates a possibility. Institutions determine whether governments can use it.

## The regional average is particularly unhelpful

There is a second problem with the 16 per cent figure.

There isn’t really a single South Asian demographic transition.

The underlying World Bank working paper examines eight countries and finds very different trajectories. Afghanistan and Pakistan are expected to continue experiencing growth in their school-age populations, while Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal and Sri Lanka face declines. [World Bank](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099120002052611106/pdf/IDU-4fde8dd8-6c02-4225-b4b7-b7a685aa192a.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Even within countries, national averages will conceal large differences.

India makes this particularly obvious. A falling national child population does not imply that every district will have fewer school-age children at the same rate. Migration, fertility differences and urbanisation will redistribute demand as well as reduce it.

That creates a planning problem.

One district may need to decide whether several small schools remain viable. Another may still need additional classrooms because families are moving into its towns and cities.

The interesting unit of analysis may therefore be much smaller than the country.

That is something I would have liked the World Bank discussion to push further. National projections tell finance ministries something important about long-run expenditure. They tell education departments much less about where the next teacher, classroom or bus will actually be needed.

## Then there is the uncomfortable question of school consolidation

Once enrolment falls, consolidation enters the conversation.

Economically, the case can be quite persuasive. Maintaining many very small schools can make it difficult to provide specialist teachers, adequate facilities and sensible pupil-teacher ratios. Combining schools could release resources for better-equipped institutions.

But a school is not simply a cost centre.

Closing a small village school can increase the distance a child must travel. That burden will not necessarily fall equally. Age, gender, disability, transport availability, household income and local geography can all affect whether a theoretically accessible school is accessible in practice.

The authors recognise this problem. They argue that consolidation requires communication with communities, safe transport and better facilities in the schools receiving additional pupils. [World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/demographic-change-is-coming-to-south-asia-s-classrooms--will-ed?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

I would put the point more strongly.

The relevant efficiency measure cannot simply be cost per student.

It has to include what happens to participation and learning after the system is reorganised.

A school closure that saves money but increases absence or dropout is not an efficiency gain in any meaningful educational sense.

## What would we do with the money?

There is an even larger question behind the paper.

Suppose the demographic transition does release resources. What should education systems buy with them?

South Asia has already made substantial progress on enrolment. The World Bank notes that primary-school entry is now close to universal across much of the region and secondary participation has risen substantially. Yet learning remains poor. In Bangladesh and India, the authors report that more than half of children completing primary school are in learning poverty, meaning they cannot read and understand a simple age-appropriate text. [World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/demographic-change-is-coming-to-south-asia-s-classrooms--will-ed?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That changes the policy problem.

The task is no longer simply:

**How do we provide schooling to a rapidly growing number of children?**

Increasingly, it becomes:

**What should we do differently when the number of children stops growing?**

The authors favour protecting education budgets and directing demographic savings towards interventions with stronger evidence of effects on learning, including better pedagogy, structured teaching support and teaching at the appropriate learning level. [World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/demographic-change-is-coming-to-south-asia-s-classrooms--will-ed?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is a sensible starting point.

But the demographic transition could also permit something less visible: more attention per child.

Better support for children who are behind. More resources for disability inclusion. Stronger early-childhood education. Better teacher development. Libraries and laboratories that function. Remedial teaching that does not disappear when a project ends.

A smaller cohort could make some of these choices more affordable.

It does not make governments choose them.

## The denominator problem

There is also a measurement issue here that I find particularly interesting.

Imagine that an education budget remains constant while the number of students declines.

Expenditure per pupil rises.

That indicator will look better.

But nothing whatsoever may have changed inside a classroom.

No teacher received better training. No textbook arrived. No child learned another word. The denominator simply became smaller.

The same problem appears when education spending is expressed as a share of GDP. The indicator is useful, but it tells us little by itself about allocation, efficiency or learning.

This is why the underlying working paper is worth reading alongside the shorter essay. The authors explicitly model education expenditure through demographic change, enrolment and spending per student under different assumptions through 2050. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1596/1813-9450-11303?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The projections are scenarios, not forecasts of what governments will actually do.

That distinction is important.

They tell us what fiscal room demographic change could create under particular assumptions. They cannot tell us whether governments will retain education budgets, reorganise schools sensibly or spend additional resources on things that improve learning.

## A demographic dividend needs politics

This is where I depart slightly from the optimistic reading of the argument.

The World Bank essay describes the demographic transition as a potential opportunity to “spend better, not just more.” [World Bank Blogs](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/demographic-change-is-coming-to-south-asia-s-classrooms--will-ed?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Perhaps.

But there is another perfectly plausible response to falling enrolment: governments reduce education budgets.

If fewer children are entering schools, finance ministries could treat declining enrolment as an opportunity to spend less rather than an opportunity to spend more per child.

Nothing in the demographic transition itself determines which response wins.

That is a political and institutional choice.

Protecting education expenditure while the number of children declines would effectively redistribute demographic savings towards the children who remain in the system. It would represent a decision to convert quantity into quality.

The demographic dividend in education is therefore rather different from the way the phrase sometimes sounds.

It is not money that automatically appears when fertility falls.

It is a choice about what to do when the denominator changes.

## What I take from this

I like this piece because it takes a demographic fact that could easily remain in the population-projection tables and asks what it means for an actual public system.

But I would read its numbers as an opening rather than a conclusion.

A smaller school-age population could create room for higher expenditure per child.

Higher expenditure per child could permit better teaching and better schools.

Better schools could improve learning.

Those are three separate propositions.

The first is largely demographic arithmetic. The second depends on budgets and institutions. The third depends on what happens inside schools.

Collapsing them into one story would make the demographic transition look far easier than it is.

For South Asian education systems, the coming decline in child populations presents an unusual opportunity. After decades spent asking how to accommodate more children, governments may finally have some room to ask what each child actually gets from school.

Whether they use that room is the question worth watching.
