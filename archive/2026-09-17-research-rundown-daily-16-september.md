## FIVE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

### 1. Bangladesh had 25 extra days of risky heat this summer because of climate change

Bangladesh experienced 31 days between June and August when temperatures exceeded the local 90th-percentile threshold. A new Climate Central attribution analysis estimates that human-caused climate change added **25 of those risky-heat days**. ([United News Bangladesh](https://unb.com.bd/category/Bangladesh/climate-change-adds-25-risky-heat-days-to-bangladeshs-summer-new-climate-central-analysis-finds/195482?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That finding matters because exposure is translating into economic loss through work. Separate research released into the policy discussion this week links extreme heat to reduced working hours, lower earnings and food insecurity, particularly among workers whose income depends directly on daily labour. A Bangladesh study by PRAAN also reports heavier financial burdens among women-headed households and points to the absence of a dedicated institutional framework for extreme heat. ([Dhaka Stream](https://www.dhakastream.net/node/198880?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The attribution estimate and the household findings measure different things and should stay separate. Climate Central estimates how climate change altered heat exposure. The household research examines consequences among selected populations. Neither, on its own, gives a national estimate of income lost to heat.

**Why it matters:** Heat policy is still often organised around temperature alerts and health emergencies. For low-income workers, the relevant outcome may be income forgone because working safely becomes impossible.

### 2. Bangladesh is preparing a Tk 4,039 crore TB, HIV and malaria programme as external financing declines

Bangladesh has prepared a three-year **Tk 4,039 crore** programme covering tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS and malaria for July 2026 to June 2029. Government financing would provide Tk 3,168.89 crore, with Tk 870.18 crore coming from the Global Fund. ([The Daily Star](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/bangladesh/news/govt-plans-tk-4039cr-drive-against-tb-hiv-malaria-4273956?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The targets are substantial: preventive TB treatment for about 1.5 million contacts of pulmonary TB patients, nutritional support for 824,450 TB patients, HIV testing across all 64 districts and zero malaria deaths by 2029. Bangladesh recorded 1,891 new HIV cases in 2025, up from 1,438 in 2024, while its malaria-elimination deadline has moved from 2030 to 2034. ([The Daily Star](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/bangladesh/news/govt-plans-tk-4039cr-drive-against-tb-hiv-malaria-4273956?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The financing context deserves attention. The previous Health, Population and Nutrition Sector Programme ended in June 2024, and declining donor support has already affected some medicine and testing services. Globally, the Global Fund says recent aid cuts have hit prevention harder than treatment, including TB case-finding and HIV prevention. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/healthcare-pharmaceuticals/aid-cuts-hit-disease-prevention-harder-than-treatment-global-fund-chief-says-2026-09-16/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This is therefore partly a disease-control programme and partly a test of whether domestic public finance can replace external health funding without losing prevention capacity.

### 3. Maharashtra now has a state climate-finance taxonomy

Maharashtra released its **State Climate Finance Framework** today, developed by the state government, MITRA and CEEW. It creates a classification and screening system for mitigation, adaptation and cross-cutting investments and is intended for use by government departments, lenders and investors. ([CEEW](https://www.ceew.in/publications/maharashtra-state-climate-finance-framework?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The interesting figure is **80 per cent**. Adaptation accounts for nearly four-fifths of Maharashtra’s proposed climate budget, according to the framework, while remaining the category least attractive to private capital because returns are difficult to monetise and investment horizons are long. ([CEEW](https://www.ceew.in/publications/maharashtra-state-climate-finance-framework?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The framework therefore gives adaptation its own four-step screening pathway, including an explicit test for maladaptation. It is also provisional. Maharashtra intends to revise it when India’s national Climate Finance Taxonomy is formally notified. ([CEEW](https://www.ceew.in/publications/maharashtra-state-climate-finance-framework?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The document is worth watching because classification affects money. Once an expenditure is labelled climate finance, the harder question is whether it produces a measurable reduction in vulnerability.

### 4. An eight-year railway delay in Bangladesh has produced a much larger bill

The proposed direct dual-gauge railway between Bogura and Sirajganj has been under development for eight years. Its revised estimated cost is now reported at about **Tk 10,391 crore**, nearly 89 per cent above the original estimate, with the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank expected to finance 71 per cent. ([The Daily Star](https://online.thedailystar.net/news/bangladesh?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There is already a data problem worth noticing. Another report today gives the revised cost as **Tk 10,242 crore**, an increase of roughly 84 per cent over the original Tk 5,579.70 crore. ([Daily Observer](https://observerbd.com/news/593592?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That discrepancy is a reason to avoid false precision until the revised Development Project Proposal is formally approved. The broader point survives either figure: long project-preparation periods impose costs through price changes, design revisions and delayed benefits.

For development evaluation, “cost escalation” also needs decomposition. Inflation, exchange-rate movements, scope changes, financing costs and genuine implementation failure are different explanations and should not be collapsed into one percentage.

### 5. Pakistan’s first fintech sandbox has produced four successful tests, but SBP says that is not regulatory approval

The State Bank of Pakistan has completed the first cohort of its regulatory sandbox. Six participants were selected for testing between January and June 2026, and four successfully demonstrated products involving inward remittances and open banking. ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/amp/2030248?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

SBP has issued an unusually useful clarification: passing the sandbox **does not constitute permission to launch commercially**. Participants still need the relevant regulatory authorisations. ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/amp/2030248?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That distinction is easy to lose when innovation programmes report the number of products that “graduate”. A sandbox establishes whether something can be tested successfully under controlled conditions. It does not establish consumer benefit, market viability, financial inclusion effects or safety at scale.

The first cohort should therefore be treated as regulatory learning rather than evidence that the products themselves have succeeded.

# ONE PAPER WORTH READING

### *No escaping the heat: health and economic impacts of heat stress on informal workers in urban Bangladesh*

This IIED study is particularly useful alongside today’s Bangladesh heat-attribution numbers. Ritu Bharadwaj and colleagues surveyed **175 informal-worker households in Dhaka**, examining workers at home and at work using the Comprehensive Climate Impact Quantification toolkit. The study attempts to count losses that standard economic statistics often miss: workdays and wages lost to heat, health expenditure, illness and other household effects. ([IIED](https://www.iied.org/22755iied?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Its contribution is measurement rather than national prevalence. Heat exposure becomes economically important before it produces hospitalisation or mortality. A worker who shortens a shift, works more slowly or stays home loses income even though none of those outcomes necessarily appears in conventional disaster-loss accounts.

The limitation follows directly from the design. **175 households in Dhaka cannot represent Bangladesh’s informal workforce**, and estimates based on reported household experience require care when translated into aggregate economic losses. Occupational exposure also differs substantially across jobs.

Still, this is exactly the sort of work needed to improve heat-loss accounting. Conventional productivity measures can miss the welfare cost when workers respond to unsafe temperatures by sacrificing either earnings or health.

**Citation:** Bharadwaj, R., N. Karthikeyan, S. Chaliha, S. Islam and U. Tanni. 2026. *No escaping the heat: health and economic impacts of heat stress on informal workers in urban Bangladesh*. London: International Institute for Environment and Development. ([IIED](https://www.iied.org/22755iied?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

# ONE NUMBER

## 25 days

The estimated number of additional risky-heat days Bangladesh experienced during June-August 2026 because of human-caused climate change. Bangladesh recorded 31 days above the locally defined 90th-percentile temperature threshold during the period. ([United News Bangladesh](https://unb.com.bd/category/Bangladesh/climate-change-adds-25-risky-heat-days-to-bangladeshs-summer-new-climate-central-analysis-finds/195482?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The denominator matters. This is **not** a claim that climate change caused 25 days that would otherwise have been cool. Attribution analysis compares the probability of temperatures under the present climate with a counterfactual climate without human-caused warming.

That makes the number useful for a different reason: it begins to separate ordinary seasonal exposure from additional exposure attributable to climate change.

# POLICY WATCH

## Maharashtra is trying to define what counts as climate finance

Climate-finance debates often start with how much money is available. Maharashtra’s new framework starts one step earlier: **which expenditure is entitled to count?**

Its taxonomy screens proposed activities as mitigation, adaptation or cross-cutting investments and includes safeguards intended to identify maladaptation. The framework says **34 of Maharashtra’s 36 districts are exposed to extreme climate events**, while a quarter of districts show changing patterns between flood and drought exposure. ([CEEW](https://www.ceew.in/publications/maharashtra-state-climate-finance-framework?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The test will come in budget practice.

A taxonomy can improve consistency without proving effectiveness. If an irrigation project qualifies as adaptation finance, for example, classification says little about whether it actually reduces vulnerability, shifts water risk elsewhere or disproportionately benefits already better-served farmers.

The useful next step would be to link climate-budget tagging to ex-post outcome measures. Otherwise a better classification system can produce a more accurate count of spending without telling us whether the spending worked.

# EDITORIAL NOTE

## Heat creates a measurement problem before it creates a disaster statistic

Today’s Bangladesh evidence points to something I think development research still handles badly.

Our systems are much better at recording events than attrition.

A cyclone destroys a house. A flood washes away a bridge. Both generate identifiable damage. Heat often works differently. A construction worker stops two hours early. A street vendor sells less. Someone works through unsafe conditions because losing the day’s wage is worse. A household spends more on water or electricity. Food expenditure falls because earnings have fallen.

Each loss can be small enough to disappear from conventional disaster accounting. Repeated across millions of workers and dozens of hot days, the aggregate may be substantial.

This is also why “productivity loss” is an incomplete outcome. If someone maintains output by continuing to work in dangerous heat, measured productivity may show little deterioration while the health cost rises.

For informal labour in South Asia, we need measures that jointly observe **exposure, hours worked, earnings and health**. Measuring only one can make adaptation look more successful than it is.

**Reader question:** What is the best existing longitudinal dataset or study that links daily heat exposure to hours worked, earnings and health outcomes for informal workers in South Asia? I am particularly interested in designs that can distinguish adaptation from workers simply absorbing greater health costs.

This belongs on the list for a future Methods Monday: how to measure labour-productivity losses from extreme heat when workers can adjust hours, effort and exposure.
