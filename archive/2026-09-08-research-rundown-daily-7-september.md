## FIVE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

### 1. Nepal’s disaster is also an infrastructure-risk story

Nepal marked a national day of mourning today as rescue operations continued after the catastrophic 26 August flood and glacier-collapse disaster. Reuters reports at least 1,380 deaths and more than 5,500 people still missing across Nepal and Tibet, with hundreds of hydropower workers among those unaccounted for.

The immediate humanitarian toll dominates, as it should. There is also a harder development question here. Hydropower, roads, settlements and border infrastructure have expanded into mountain systems where cryosphere and cascading-hazard risks are changing. The event should prompt scrutiny of what assumptions about hazard frequency and magnitude sit inside infrastructure appraisal.

Why it matters: adaptation cannot remain an environmental add-on to infrastructure policy when the infrastructure itself changes exposure.

### 2. Bangladesh’s dengue outbreak is accelerating

Bangladesh has recorded 42,590 dengue hospitalisations and 117 deaths so far in 2026, according to figures reported by Reuters. The latest 24-hour reporting period produced 1,558 admissions, the highest daily number this year, while hospitals in Dhaka and elsewhere are facing growing pressure.

The trajectory matters as much as the cumulative count. Weather remains favourable for *Aedes* transmission, and the health system is simultaneously managing a serious measles outbreak. Fogging and generic clean-up campaigns are poor substitutes for surveillance-led vector control when transmission is already widespread.

Why it matters: Bangladesh is entering the part of the dengue season where hospital capacity, case management and geographically targeted vector control need to be read together.

### 3. South Asia may have an unusually strong economic case for clean-air investment

A new assessment from the UN Environment Programme and Climate and Clean Air Coalition estimates that every US$1 spent globally on integrated climate and clean-air measures could generate around US$15 in economic benefits. The South Asian result is even larger: the assessment estimates the region’s existing air-pollution burden at roughly 10 per cent of GDP and a benefit-cost ratio of about 21:1 for the package it models.

Those are modelled estimates, rather than observed programme returns, and should be read accordingly. Still, the exercise is useful because it puts mortality, morbidity, productivity and climate effects into the same economic accounting rather than treating air pollution as a narrow environmental externality.

Why it matters: for South Asian finance ministries, clean-air policy increasingly looks like economic policy with measurable health returns.

### 4. Heat and wildfire smoke are complicating the air-quality story

The World Meteorological Organization’s new *Air Quality and Climate Bulletin* argues that heatwaves and wildfires can reverse gains from reductions in conventional industrial and transport pollution. The report focuses on PM2.5, ground-level ozone, black carbon and other aerosols, and calls for much better atmospheric observation. Northern India appears explicitly among the regions where local and regional pollution processes interact with climate.

This matters for measurement. Annual city averages can conceal changing pollutant composition, episodic exposure and regional transport. A monitoring system designed mainly around compliance with fixed concentration thresholds may tell us too little about where exposure is coming from.

Why it matters: climate change is altering the production and movement of air pollution, which changes what governments need to measure as well as what they need to regulate.

### 5. India’s factory slowdown has reached employment

India’s manufacturing PMI fell to 52.8 in August, its weakest reading in five years, alongside slower output and new orders. More consequentially for the labour story, surveyed manufacturers reported falling employment for the first time in roughly two and a half years.

A diffusion index is a high-frequency business indicator, not a substitute for PLFS employment estimates, and month-to-month movement deserves restraint. The employment component is still worth watching because it sits beside a wider question about whether industrial expansion is producing enough jobs.

Why it matters: if subsequent labour and industrial data confirm the signal, the slowdown has moved beyond production momentum into employment.

## ONE PAPER WORTH READING

### Hidden Assets: The Economic and Health Case for Climate and Clean Air Action

The most useful new research product today is UNEP and the Climate and Clean Air Coalition’s global economic assessment of joint climate and air-pollution policy. It asks a practical question: what changes when the economic appraisal counts climate mitigation and clean-air benefits together?

The assessment models 25 measures across energy, industry, transport, agriculture and food systems, household energy and waste. It incorporates implementation costs alongside market gains and health-related welfare benefits. Its headline estimate is striking: integrated action produces roughly US$15 in benefits for every US$1 invested globally, with market benefits alone exceeding costs within ten years. For South Asia, the estimated return rises to about 21:1.

The caution is substantial. These are model-derived social returns across a package of interventions. They are sensitive to assumptions about valuation of mortality and morbidity, implementation speed, baseline emissions and future policy. A benefit-cost ratio of 21:1 should therefore be read as an estimate from a defined modelling framework, rather than a forecast of fiscal returns available to any individual government programme.

The report still does something useful. It makes the opportunity cost of treating air quality, health and climate as separate administrative problems empirically visible.

**Citation:** United Nations Environment Programme and Climate and Clean Air Coalition. 2026. *Hidden Assets: The Economic and Health Case for Climate and Clean Air Action*. Nairobi: UNEP.

## ONE NUMBER

### 10%

That is the estimated current economic burden of air pollution in South Asia as a share of regional GDP in the new UNEP-CCAC assessment. It is the highest burden among the regions assessed.

The number includes monetised health and wider economic damages, so it should not be interpreted as ten percentage points of measured GDP disappearing from national accounts. Its value is comparative: once health losses are priced, dirty air is large enough to sit alongside the macroeconomic variables that routinely receive far more policy attention.

## POLICY WATCH

### India gives employers another route to settle old provident-fund disputes

EPFO is urging eligible establishments to use VISHWAS 2026 to settle long-running disputes over damages for delayed provident-fund remittances at reduced rates. The scheme covers categories including pending litigation, unpaid final orders and some pre-adjudication cases.

The policy question is worth separating from the administrative one. Lower penalties can clear litigation and recover outstanding dues faster. They can also weaken deterrence if repeated amnesties teach employers that delayed compliance will eventually become cheaper. The test is therefore what happens to recovery times, fresh defaults and workers’ credited contributions after the scheme, rather than the number of cases formally closed.

## EDITORIAL NOTE

### Climate adaptation needs a different denominator

The Nepal disaster exposes a recurring weakness in adaptation debates. We count projects protected, warnings issued, kilometres of embankment built and money allocated. Those measures say little about whether the underlying distribution of risk has changed.

Infrastructure creates its own exposure. A hydropower project places workers, tunnels, access roads, transmission assets and settlements inside a hazard system. A new road changes where people build. Urban drainage alters land values and settlement patterns. Adaptation appraisal therefore needs to ask how development itself changes the population and assets exposed to a hazard over the lifetime of an investment.

This also complicates attribution. The immediate physical cause of an individual disaster can remain uncertain while the policy conclusion is already clear: historical hazard distributions are becoming less reliable guides to future risk in a warming mountain system.

For South Asia, that is a planning problem before it is a climate-finance slogan.
