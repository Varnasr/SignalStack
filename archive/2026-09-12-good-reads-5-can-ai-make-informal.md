*Good Reads #5 AI and informal work*

There is a useful reversal at the beginning of Anthony Tan’s essay in the September issue of Finance & Development. Much of the discussion about artificial intelligence begins with formal employment: which jobs will disappear, which occupations will change, and which workers will become more productive. Tan starts elsewhere.

In Southeast Asia, he writes, 78 per cent of workers operate in the informal economy. If that is where most people work, then asking what AI will do to salaried employment captures only part of the development question. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/point-of-view-ai-and-the-informal-economy-anthony-tan?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

It is a sensible place to begin.

Tan’s larger argument is more contentious. Informality, he suggests, should not simply be understood as a temporary condition from which workers must eventually graduate. Digital platforms can instead make informal work function better. They can use transaction and earnings data to extend credit to people banks do not serve, match workers more efficiently with demand, provide training, and give small businesses tools that would previously have been available mainly to larger firms.

The examples are striking. Tan reports that Grab disbursed $3.2 billion in loans to drivers and merchants last year, with 68 per cent of borrowers gaining access to formal credit for the first time. More than 1.5 million drivers completed at least one GrabAcademy course. One in four micro, small and medium enterprises on the platform uses its AI assistant, which Grab associates with a 15 per cent increase in sales. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/point-of-view-ai-and-the-informal-economy-anthony-tan?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

These numbers deserve attention. They also deserve scrutiny.

Tan is the chief executive and cofounder of Grab. The essay is therefore not an independent assessment of whether platformisation improves informal work. It is an argument from one of the companies building the system being described. The distinction matters.

The central question is not whether platforms can provide useful services. Plainly, they can. The question is what kind of labour market is created when access to customers, credit, training, information and eventually social protection increasingly passes through privately governed platforms.

That changes the development problem.

A worker may gain access to credit because a platform can observe earnings that a bank cannot. But the same arrangement gives the platform an unusually detailed view of that worker’s economic life. An algorithm may reduce the time a driver spends waiting for the next passenger. It can also influence where the driver goes, which work appears worthwhile and how income opportunities are distributed.

The platform is no longer merely matching buyer and seller. It begins to perform functions previously divided among markets, employers, financial institutions and, in some cases, the state.

Tan recognises part of the social-protection problem. He argues for benefits that travel with workers across jobs and platforms rather than depending upon their classification as employees. He points to Malaysia’s 2026 Gig Workers Act as an example of extending social-security coverage without making gig workers employees. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/point-of-view-ai-and-the-informal-economy-anthony-tan?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That is an interesting direction. But it leaves the political economy of platform power largely outside the frame.

There is another claim worth examining carefully. Tan argues that policy should be designed around informality rather than treating formalisation as the principal measure of progress. There is something persuasive here. Development policy has often treated the informal economy as a residual category, even when it contains most of the workforce.

Yet accepting informality as persistent is not the same thing as accepting its present terms.

For India, that distinction is especially useful. The relevant question is unlikely to be whether digital technology will somehow eliminate informal work. It is whether technology can raise productivity and incomes while workers also gain bargaining power, social insurance, predictable rules and meaningful control over the data through which increasingly large parts of their working lives are organised.

That makes this less an essay about AI than it first appears.

It is really an argument about what development policy should do when informality persists and technology becomes one of the institutions governing it.

Tan offers one answer: design around the economy people actually inhabit.

I think that is the right starting point. I am much less certain that privately governed platforms can be treated simply as the means of getting there.

### why read it?

Because it shifts the AI discussion away from the formal jobs that dominate much of the debate and towards the economic lives of workers in developing countries. And because its blind spots are almost as useful as its argument.

The essay makes a strong case for taking informality seriously. It also gives us a good reason to ask who should control the digital infrastructure increasingly being built around it.
