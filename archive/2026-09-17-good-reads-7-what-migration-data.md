*Good Reads#7 on 17th September 2026*

[Read Nehal Ahmed, “Ranglal, hamari koi chitthi aayi hai?” in The Migration Story](https://themigrationstory.com/post/ranglal-hamari-koi-chitthi-aayi-hai)

There is a moment near the end of Nehal Ahmed’s essay when Ranglal, the village postman, appears.

For years, people in the village would see him cycling past and ask whether a letter had arrived from someone working away in the Gulf. Ranglal delivered letters and money orders. He also read letters for people who could not read them and wrote replies for those who could not write. When there was no letter, people sometimes told him what they had wanted to tell the absent person anyway. ([The Migration Story](https://themigrationstory.com/post/ranglal-hamari-koi-chitthi-aayi-hai))

It is a lovely detail. It is also, from a researcher’s point of view, an unusually useful one.

Much of what we know about migration comes from questions that are necessarily easier to count. Who migrated? Where did they go? For how long? What work did they do? How much did they earn? Did they send money home?

Ahmed’s essay asks us, without quite putting it this way, what disappears when those become our principal categories for understanding migration.

## Migration is also an infrastructure of absence

The essay follows several generations of Gulf migration from Ahmed’s village in Bihar through the technologies families used to remain connected: letters carried by other migrants, landlines, audio cassettes, cameras and film rolls, calling systems, early mobile phones, cyber cafés and eventually smartphones, WhatsApp and video calls. His own father worked in Saudi Arabia. By the time later generations migrated, both the work migrants did and the technologies connecting them to home had changed. ([The Migration Story](https://themigrationstory.com/post/ranglal-hamari-koi-chitthi-aayi-hai))

Read simply as a history of communications technology, this is interesting enough.

But the technology is really a way of observing something else: how migration reorganises family life.

A migrant can be economically present in a household while being physically absent from it. Money travels home. Decisions are made across enormous distances. Children grow up between visits. Marriages are conducted partly through correspondence. Photographs become records of years somebody did not witness directly.

The cost of migration, then, cannot be reduced to the cost of moving or the conditions of employment at the destination.

Distance itself has consequences.

## Who controls the telephone matters

One of the strongest parts of the essay concerns gender.

Landlines eventually made communication faster, but Ahmed recalls that they were commonly situated in the men’s section of the household. For young married women living with their husbands’ families while those husbands worked abroad, access to a telephone did not necessarily mean access to a private conversation.

Letters could offer something the supposedly superior technology could not: privacy.

Audio cassettes extended that private space further. A woman could record a message away from the household and send it to her husband. Ahmed places this alongside the better-known cassette culture associated with Gulf migration from Kerala. ([The Migration Story](https://themigrationstory.com/post/ranglal-hamari-koi-chitthi-aayi-hai))

This is a small but important methodological lesson.

If we measured communication by asking whether a household had access to a telephone, we could easily record an improvement. The household had acquired the technology. Contact had become technically possible.

But access at household level tells us very little about who can actually use a technology, under what conditions, and with whom.

Those are different variables.

It is the sort of distinction that matters well beyond this particular story. Household ownership is often a poor proxy for individual control. A phone, bank account, vehicle or internet connection can exist within a household while access to it remains structured by gender, age, income and authority.

## Technology changes distance. It does not abolish it.

Ahmed describes another transition that will be familiar to many migrant families.

With cheap internet and smartphones, communication becomes frequent and immediate. Migrants can see their children rather than discovering, on returning home after years away, how much they have grown. Families exchange photographs, messages, voice notes and video calls and can remain involved in ordinary daily life from thousands of kilometres away. ([The Migration Story](https://themigrationstory.com/post/ranglal-hamari-koi-chitthi-aayi-hai))

Something has plainly improved.

But I found myself wondering what exactly we would measure if we wanted to understand that improvement.

Frequency of contact is possible. Cost of communication is possible. Smartphone ownership is possible. Internet access is possible.

None quite captures the change Ahmed is describing.

What has changed is partly the experience of absence.

That is much harder to put into a survey variable.

## The things surveys flatten

This is why I wanted to include this piece in Good Reads.

It is not a research paper and should not be made to pretend to be one. Ahmed is writing from family memory and personal experience, supported by photographs, letters and the history of his village. It cannot tell us how representative these experiences were across Bihar, much less across India’s many migration corridors.

That is precisely why it is useful alongside quantitative research rather than in competition with it.

A migration survey can estimate prevalence and patterns. Administrative records can tell us about passports, borders, transfers or employment. Household surveys can examine remittances and consumption. Longitudinal work can follow changes over time.

But the categories we choose determine what becomes visible.

If the principal economic story of migration is that a worker moves from a place with fewer opportunities to one with better-paid work and sends income home, we can miss much of the institution that forms around that movement.

Someone carries the letter.

Someone waits for the call.

Someone controls the household telephone.

Someone writes on behalf of the person who cannot write.

Someone raises the children who remain behind.

Someone learns how to operate the cyber café.

Someone keeps the photograph.

These are not decorative details around the economic fact of migration. They are part of how migration works.

## And then there is Ranglal

By the time Ahmed reaches Ranglal, communications technology has changed almost beyond recognition.

The postman who once connected migrant families has disappeared, and almost no visual record of him remains. Years later, when Ahmed’s brother tells his own daughters about Ranglal, one of them asks what he looked like. She reconstructs an image of him on an iPad from her father’s description. It becomes the family’s only visual representation of a man who once carried so much of the village’s communication. ([The Migration Story](https://themigrationstory.com/post/ranglal-hamari-koi-chitthi-aayi-hai))

There is an obvious irony in that ending, and Ahmed lets it stand.

For me, it also raises a question about evidence.

We tend to think of the digital era as producing an extraordinary abundance of records. Earlier everyday lives can look sparse by comparison. Yet what survives has never been a neutral sample of what mattered.

Ranglal mattered enormously and left almost no archive.

That problem should be familiar to researchers. What is recorded is not necessarily what was important. What is measurable is not necessarily what was consequential. And an absence in the data does not mean an absence in social life.

Good research needs the survey and the story.

Sometimes the story tells us what we forgot to ask.
