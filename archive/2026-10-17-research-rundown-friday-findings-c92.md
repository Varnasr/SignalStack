*Friday Findings October 9 2026*

This week's papers examine several familiar assumptions in development policy. An increase in rural nonfarm employment may coexist with persistent labour-market segmentation. Training women entrepreneurs can improve their access to corporate procurement, although previous experience influences who secures contracts. Even brief delays on educational platforms appear to affect learning.

Research from Nepal, Bangladesh and Manipur adds evidence about environmental exposure, food systems and the use of geographical information for public decisions. The selection also includes two studies with implications for public health and land-use policy.

Eight papers are worth examining this week.

## 1. Rural nonfarm employment in India is expanding, with unequal returns.

Shadab Hashmi and Abdul Latheef K examine rural nonfarm employment using nationally representative microdata from India's Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), 2021–22. Their paper, published in *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications* on 8 October, investigates wage differences between agricultural and non-agricultural employment and the characteristics associated with access to better-paying work.

The authors combine a Mincerian wage regression with quantile regressions and a Heckman two-step selection model. This allows them to examine average wage differences, variation across the conditional wage distribution and potential selection into different forms of employment.

They find a positive average nonfarm wage premium. The estimated premium declines towards the upper end of the wage distribution, however, and access to better-paying nonfarm work remains associated with education, technical training, formal employment and geographical location. Women, informal workers and socially disadvantaged groups experience persistent disadvantages.

These findings complicate the interpretation of structural transformation. An increase in employment outside agriculture provides limited information about whether workers have moved into more productive or secure occupations. The distinction is especially relevant where workers enter casual construction, petty trade and other activities offering irregular earnings.

The paper has methodological limitations. Its estimates come from cross-sectional data, and the Heckman correction depends on assumptions about selection and model specification. A statistically significant selection term does not establish why workers entered particular occupations. The estimated wage premium should therefore be interpreted as a conditional association.

The authors' larger claim about changes in employment quality over time also requires evidence from comparable survey rounds.

I would recommend this paper to researchers working on employment diversification, rural livelihoods and structural transformation. Its strongest contribution is the attention it gives to differences within nonfarm employment, particularly the unequal returns associated with education, gender and social position.

**Paper:** Hashmi, Shadab, and Abdul Latheef K. 2026. “Rural Nonfarm Employment in India: Structural Transformation, Wage Determinants, and Labour Market Segmentation.” *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*. Published 8 October.

[Read the open-access paper](https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-026-09283-5).

## 2. Procurement training helps women-owned firms bid for contracts. Previous experience influences who wins.

Rachel Cassidy, David McKenzie and Kalyan Neelamraju examine whether targeted procurement training can help women-owned and women-led enterprises enter corporate supply chains.

Their World Bank working paper, released on 7 October, reports findings from a phased randomised controlled trial in Colombia. The programme provided procurement training through masterclasses, bootcamps and mentoring, with outcomes measured at baseline and approximately six and twelve months later.

The intervention increased registration on corporate procurement portals by ten percentage points and the likelihood of submitting a bid by eight percentage points.

The effects on winning contracts were concentrated among firms with previous procurement knowledge or experience. For these enterprises, the probability of winning a contract increased by approximately eight to nine percentage points, representing around 20 per cent of the control-group mean.

The distinction between entering a procurement process and winning a contract is important. Registration and bid submission are intermediate outcomes. Contract acquisition depends on additional factors, including the firm's production capacity, commercial experience and ability to meet buyer requirements.

The study also has an interesting methodological contribution. Participation occurred through several stages, and some enterprises assigned to the programme did not complete all its components. The authors use complier-weighted treatment effects to examine outcomes under these conditions, alongside intention-to-treat estimates and sensitivity analyses.

This is relevant to enterprise-development evaluations where enrolment, attendance, completion and subsequent business activity form a sequence of decisions.

The experiment provides credible causal evidence within the Colombian programme. Its results should be applied cautiously to other settings. Procurement practices, buyer behaviour and the characteristics of participating enterprises may differ substantially.

For India, the findings are relevant to efforts to increase women's participation in government procurement and corporate supply chains. They suggest that training may improve entry into procurement systems, while the benefits of winning contracts could depend heavily on firms' existing capabilities.

I would place this among the week's strongest papers because the research design permits causal interpretation and the outcomes extend beyond knowledge gained during training.

**Paper:** Cassidy, Rachel, David McKenzie, and Kalyan N. Neelamraju. 2026. “Breaking into Supply Chains: Experimental Evidence on Procurement Training for Women-Owned SMEs.” World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11481. Published 7 October.

[Read the working paper](https://ideas.repec.org/p/wbk/wbrwps/11481.html).

[Access the reproducibility materials](https://reproducibility.worldbank.org/catalog/653).

## 3. A two-second delay can affect online learning.

Asaf Mazar and colleagues examine a problem that educational technology evaluations frequently overlook: the effect of brief loading delays during online learning.

Their paper, published in *Nature Human Behaviour* on 8 October, combines two longitudinal quasi-experimental field studies.

The first uses observations from 26,707 American primary and secondary school students completing online mathematics assignments. The second examines 55,184 aspiring programmers, primarily in Latin America, using an online coding platform.

Across both settings, loading delays of approximately one to two seconds were associated with poorer engagement.

Students experiencing these delays completed fewer assignments, took longer to finish their work, became distracted more frequently and obtained lower scores. Aspiring programmers spent less time actively engaged, completed fewer lessons and were less likely to reach an important course milestone.

The authors exploit variation in loading times experienced by the same users and test the findings across alternative specifications. This provides stronger evidence than a simple comparison between users with fast and slow internet connections, since those groups may differ in income, education and other characteristics.

There are limits to the causal interpretation. Loading times were observed in functioning platforms under real-world conditions, and the studies rely substantially on within-person variation rather than random assignment of delays. Unobserved conditions that vary over time could still influence the estimates.

The findings are particularly relevant to educational technology programmes in countries where internet access is uneven.

Evaluations often record whether schools and students have connectivity, how frequently a platform is used and whether users complete assigned activities. The quality of the connection during individual learning tasks receives much less attention.

This paper suggests that technical performance should be treated as a potential determinant of learning outcomes. Platform design, responsiveness and network quality may influence whether students remain engaged.

I would recommend it to researchers evaluating digital learning interventions. It offers an empirically grounded reason to measure the actual user experience alongside access to devices and connectivity.

**Paper:** Mazar, Asaf, Geoff Tomaino, Abubakir Siedahmed, and colleagues. 2026. “Microdelays Disrupt Online Learning.” *Nature Human Behaviour*. Published 8 October.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02598-y).

## 4. Himalayan flood exposure is increasing, and the underlying building data need careful validation.

C. Scott Watson and colleagues examine changes in the exposure of buildings and populations to potential flooding in Nepal and adjoining Himalayan catchments in India and China.

Their paper, published in *Scientific Reports* on 8 October, combines satellite-derived building inventories, terrain information and modelled glacial lake outburst flood inundation areas.

An important part of the study concerns the accuracy of the underlying geographical datasets. The authors compare five building-footprint products against manually identified reference data. Google Open Buildings Temporal 2.5D performed best, with a median F1 score of 0.68, followed by Google Open Buildings V3 at 0.62.

These values indicate that substantial classification errors remain even in the better-performing datasets. Differences between products can materially affect estimates of the buildings exposed to hazards.

Using the selected temporal dataset, the researchers estimate that building area within fifty metres of vertical elevation above nearby drainage channels increased by 46 per cent between 2016 and 2023. Building area within modelled glacial lake outburst flood inundation zones increased by 77 per cent.

The authors estimate that approximately 293,800 people, representing 18 per cent of the population in their study-area catchments, lived within the specified fifty-metre vertical proximity zone in 2026.

The meaning of exposure requires care. The height-above-nearest-drainage measure identifies geographical proximity in elevation to drainage channels. It does not represent an observed flood event or directly estimate the probability of flooding at each building.

The population figures also rely on projections and are less certain than the building estimates.

The methodological contribution is substantial. Disaster-risk assessments increasingly use remotely sensed settlement data, often without adequately examining their accuracy in mountainous terrain. Errors in building identification or positioning can alter exposure estimates considerably.

This study demonstrates why validation should precede the use of such datasets for investment and disaster planning.

For Himalayan states and districts, the research offers useful methods for examining settlement expansion in areas potentially exposed to floods. The next step would be to combine these data with infrastructure condition, household vulnerability and the consequences of previous flood events.

**Paper:** Watson, C. Scott, Lauren D. Rawlins, Mohan B. Chand, and colleagues. 2026. “Increasing Exposure to Potential Flooding in High-Mountain Catchments.” *Scientific Reports*. Published 8 October.

[Read the open-access paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-70268-0).

## 5. Personalised risk messages increased influenza vaccination in three randomised trials.

Gail Rosenbaum and colleagues examine whether personalised information about health risks can influence vaccination decisions.

Their paper, published in *Nature Human Behaviour* on 5 October, reports three preregistered randomised field trials involving more than 90,000 unique patients in a United States healthcare system.

The researchers used an existing machine-learning model to identify patients at elevated risk of influenza and related complications. Participants were randomly assigned to receive no message or one of several vaccination reminders.

Some messages provided personalised information about elevated risk. Others disclosed that an algorithm had identified the patient's risk level or explained the factors contributing to that assessment.

The personalised high-risk messages increased vaccination by approximately 1.1 to 1.4 percentage points compared with ordinary reminders. Relative to patients who received no message, the increases ranged from 1.7 to 3.5 percentage points.

The researchers found little evidence that explicitly mentioning the algorithm or explaining its prediction produced additional improvements in vaccination uptake.

These are modest absolute effects, although their public health significance depends partly on the size and characteristics of the population reached and the costs of delivering the messages.

The randomised design supports causal interpretation of the messages' effects on recorded vaccination within the study population. The findings are less informative about how patients interpreted the messages or why particular versions worked.

The trial also took place within a healthcare system capable of identifying high-risk patients through existing electronic records. Its applicability to settings with incomplete patient information requires further examination.

For public health programmes in India, the study raises practical questions about targeting and communication. The effectiveness of personalised messages would depend on the accuracy of the underlying risk information, the ability to contact patients and the availability of vaccination services.

The paper is worth reading for its experimental design and its separation of message content from the use of an algorithm to select recipients.

**Paper:** Rosenbaum, Gail M., Amir Goren, Maheen Shermohammed, and colleagues. 2026. “Algorithm-Assisted Personalized Risk Communication to Encourage Flu Vaccination in the USA: Three Randomized Field Trials.” *Nature Human Behaviour*. Published 5 October.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02603-4).

## 6. Home gardens remain part of urban food systems in Imphal.

Arati Ningombam and colleagues examine urban and peri-urban agriculture in Imphal, Manipur, in a paper published in *Scientific Reports* on 6 October.

The study uses a mixed-methods survey of 150 households to investigate household food production, gardening practices and the relationship between these activities and local food traditions.

The researchers describe how traditional homestead gardens have changed as residential density has increased. Households increasingly use smaller spaces, including terraces, rooftops and containers, to produce vegetables and herbs.

Women manage most of the gardens examined in the study. The authors also document concerns about pesticide residues in commercially purchased vegetables as one reason households pursue home cultivation.

These findings provide useful information about the persistence of household food production within an increasingly urban environment.

The study's conclusions about nutritional security require more caution. Growing vegetables at home may contribute to dietary diversity, but the extent of that contribution depends on production quantities, seasonal availability, household consumption and the foods displaced or supplemented.

The research design cannot establish that household gardening caused improvements in nutritional status. The sample is also too small to support broad claims about the prevalence of gardening across Imphal without further evidence about how participating households were selected.

The authors' wider claims concerning social resilience should be read in the same way.

The paper is nevertheless valuable for documenting a form of urban food production that can be overlooked in conventional agricultural statistics.

There are also questions worth pursuing through further research. The economic value of home-produced food could be estimated alongside household food purchases, women's time use and the availability of space and water.

I would recommend the paper to researchers working on urban food systems and household nutrition, particularly those interested in combining survey methods with qualitative research. Its descriptive evidence is useful, while its claims about nutritional and social outcomes need stronger testing.

**Paper:** Ningombam, Arati, Blessa Sailo, Sonia Chongtham, and colleagues. 2026. “Role of Urban and Peri-Urban Agriculture in Nutritional Diversity, Cultural Resilience and Community-Based Food Security in an Eastern Himalayan City.” *Scientific Reports*. Published 6 October.

[Read the open-access paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-72784-5).

## 7. Researchers identify limited suitable habitat for fish sanctuaries in three major river basins of Bangladesh.

Murad Ahmed Farukh and colleagues examine habitat suitability across the Ganges–Padma, Brahmaputra–Jamuna and Barak–Meghna river systems in Bangladesh.

Their study, published in *Scientific Reports* on 9 October, combines satellite-derived land-use information, seasonal water observations, hydrochemical measurements and geographical multi-criteria analysis.

The researchers examine changes in mapped land cover between 2000 and 2020, together with indicators of water quality and habitat conditions.

They report a 1.5 per cent decline in mapped monsoon water extent and a 12.7 per cent decline in the monsoon vegetation class, alongside a 25 per cent increase in dry-season vegetation.

Water quality presents additional constraints. Approximately 37.5 per cent of site-season observations were classified as poor, very poor or unsuitable. The measurements included low dissolved oxygen and elevated concentrations of particular pollutants.

The resulting ecological suitability index classified only 4.9 per cent of the mapped area as highly suitable for fish sanctuaries.

The analysis is relevant to fisheries management because the suitability of a sanctuary depends on several conditions operating together. Habitat connectivity, seasonal water availability and pollution can influence whether protected areas provide appropriate ecological conditions.

The study also demonstrates a limitation of spatial decision-support models. Their outputs depend on the indicators selected, the weights assigned to those indicators and the quality of the underlying measurements.

A classification of highly suitable habitat should therefore be validated against ecological observations, including fish distribution and breeding outcomes where data permit.

The research could inform sanctuary planning, although management decisions also need to consider fishing livelihoods, seasonal access and local institutional arrangements.

I would recommend this paper to researchers interested in combining remote sensing, environmental monitoring and spatial planning. Its methods are potentially transferable to other South Asian river systems, subject to local validation.

**Paper:** Farukh, Murad Ahmed, M. Zulfikar Rahman, Md. Abdus Salam, and colleagues. 2026. “Integrating Land-Use Dynamics, Water Quality, and Multi-Criteria Ecological Suitability for Fish Sanctuary Prioritization in Three Major River Basins of Bangladesh.” *Scientific Reports*. Published 9 October.

[Read the open-access paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-72239-x).

## 8. Reclaiming wetlands for agriculture delivers limited production gains while increasing flood exposure.

Xi Zhang and colleagues examine the agricultural benefits and flood exposure associated with converting wetlands into cropland.

Their paper, published in *Nature Sustainability* on 9 October, identifies wetland-reclaimed cropland globally between 2000 and 2022 and compares its production performance and flood exposure with existing agricultural land.

The findings are substantial. Nearly half of reclaimed wetland cropland had lower yields than existing cropland. The reclaimed areas contributed approximately 0.1 per cent of total global crop production.

More than half of the reclaimed cropland faced severe flood hazards, with an average flood exposure probability estimated to be twenty-three times that of cropland generally.

The authors classify approximately 60 per cent of reclaimed wetland cropland as experiencing the combined disadvantages of limited agricultural gains and high flood exposure.

These findings challenge the economic justification for agricultural expansion through wetland conversion in many locations.

The study is based on geographical analysis and modelled estimates. Its conclusions depend on the accuracy of wetland classification, crop production estimates and flood exposure measures. The results should therefore be interpreted as evidence about patterns across mapped locations.

They do not establish the financial returns or welfare consequences for every individual farming household cultivating reclaimed land.

The policy implications extend to South Asia, where wetlands support agriculture, fisheries, flood regulation and other economic activities.

Assessing conversion proposals requires information about agricultural output and the services lost through changes in land use. Flood exposure is especially relevant where communities already experience frequent inundation.

The paper provides evidence that many reclamation projects may generate limited agricultural gains while exposing production to substantial environmental risk.

I would recommend this study to researchers working on land-use policy, agricultural economics and climate adaptation. Its value lies in comparing agricultural gains with environmental exposure within a common geographical framework.

**Paper:** Zhang, Xi, Jinwei Dong, Yiqun Shang, and colleagues. 2026. “Limited Agricultural Gains but High Flood Risk from Global Wetland Reclamation.” *Nature Sustainability*. Published 9 October.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-026-01965-x).

## What I would read first

The procurement experiment by Cassidy, McKenzie and Neelamraju is my first recommendation this week. It has a credible experimental design, reports outcomes extending to actual procurement behaviour, and addresses a problem common to enterprise-development programmes. The distinction between programme assignment, participation and eventual commercial outcomes is particularly useful for evaluation design.

Hashmi and Latheef's paper on rural nonfarm employment deserves attention for a different reason. It questions what can be inferred from changes in employment categories and directs attention towards earnings, labour-market segmentation and selection. The cross-sectional design limits causal claims, but the distributional analysis raises worthwhile questions.

The Himalayan flood-exposure study is especially relevant for researchers using geospatial data. Its validation exercise demonstrates how differences between building datasets affect the apparent distribution of risk.

I would also keep the online-learning paper close at hand. Its findings suggest that brief interruptions during digital learning may have measurable consequences for engagement and achievement, an issue that deserves greater attention in educational technology evaluations.

The remaining papers are useful for more specialised research interests. The Imphal study is primarily descriptive, the Bangladesh paper offers a spatial planning method, and the wetland study provides a comparative assessment of agricultural gains and environmental exposure. The influenza vaccination trials contribute credible experimental evidence about personalised health communication.

Across the eight papers, the central methodological lesson concerns the relationship between the outcome being measured and the conclusion that can be supported. Registration, employment category, geographical exposure and programme participation each describe a particular condition or activity. Their consequences for income, welfare, learning or health require additional evidence.
