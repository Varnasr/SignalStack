1.  Bhattacharya, Debapriya, Towfiqul Islam Khan, Mamtajul Jannat, and Maliha Rahman. 2026. *Is Bangladesh Ready for the Future of Work: Preparing the Labour Market for Automation, AI, and Structural Transition*. Centre for Policy Dialogue.

Released online on 6 September, although the report itself carries a July 2026 publication period. CPD estimates that automation could place up to 1.22 million ready-made garment jobs at risk and that as much as 60 per cent of women’s RMG employment could be displaced by 2041. More useful than the headline number is the attempt to ask what different labour-market futures might look like. The authors identify 27 drivers of change and construct four scenarios for 2035.

The obvious caution is that these are foresight estimates. They depend heavily on assumptions about adoption, sectoral adjustment, skills and institutional response. They should not be read as forecasts of job losses. For WEE research, however, the gender composition of Bangladesh’s garment workforce makes the exercise worth close attention.

1.  Taluja, Kajal, Shailja Shah, Arup Saha, et al. 2026. “Stool Testing for Tuberculosis Diagnosis in Pregnant Women and Under Five Children: A Community Based Mixed Method Study from Rural India.” medRxiv preprint. doi: 10.64898/2026.09.03.26362132.

An unusual and potentially useful diagnostic study from two districts in Bihar. The researchers worked within primary-care settings and offered Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra stool testing to pregnant women and children under five who could not produce sputum. Stool testing identified 21 TB cases. The accompanying qualitative work identified predictable operational problems: sample handling, transport, stigma and low awareness among patients and providers.

The study has direct programme relevance because sputum collection is a real barrier in these populations. The evidence remains preliminary. The number of detected cases is small, the study covers two districts, and there is no basis here for assuming comparable diagnostic yield across NTEP settings. It is also a preprint.

Still, this is exactly the kind of implementation question public-health research often neglects: whether an available diagnostic method can actually work inside ordinary primary-care systems.

1.  National Planning Commission and National Disaster Risk Reduction and Management Authority, Nepal. 2026. Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment of the August Bhotekoshi floods.

Nepal’s preliminary assessment now puts recovery and reconstruction needs at NPR 723.31 billion. Infrastructure accounts for NPR 473.57 billion. Thirteen hydropower projects, five solar plants, roads, bridges, homes, schools, health facilities and roughly 1,806 hectares of crops were affected. The assessment combines administrative data, sectoral estimates, comparison of pre- and post-disaster statistics and drone surveys.

These numbers deserve caution. This is an RDNA, and Nepal is simultaneously preparing the fuller post-disaster needs assessment. Estimates may move substantially as asset damage, livelihood losses and reconstruction standards are revised.

The larger research question is already clear. A climate disaster of this scale changes the development budget itself. When hydropower, roads and productive assets are repeatedly rebuilt, adaptation becomes a fiscal and macroeconomic problem rather than a separate environmental programme.

This follows directly from the Nepal flood material covered in the Daily earlier this week. I would return to the reconstruction estimates once the PDNA appears.

1.  Government of Bangladesh, with technical support from ILO and SANEM. 2026. Just Transition chapter and implementation framework under Bangladesh’s NDC 3.0.

Bangladesh has now attached an investment framework to its just-transition commitments. The chapter maps 258 commitments across 14 national policy documents into 33 priorities and 69 activities covering energy, transport, construction, agriculture, waste and industrial processes. The stated investment envelope is $1.85 billion. Only $250.6 million represents direct programme costs; about $1.6 billion is expected to come through commercial and development finance. Nearly 200 representatives participated across six rounds of consultation.

This is a policy architecture rather than an evaluation. Its value lies in making several things measurable: responsible institutions, budgets, timelines and indicators. Whether the framework protects workers during sectoral change will depend on the employment and distributional analysis underneath those activities.

For South Asian climate policy, this deserves watching because “just transition” often remains a statement of intent. Bangladesh is beginning to specify who does what, when and at what cost.

1.  Bhardwaj, Aditya Vardhan. 2026. “The Carbon Credit Case for Nano Urea.” *Ideas for India*, 7 September 2026.

Bhardwaj proposes using India’s Carbon Credit Trading Scheme to pay farmers for verified reductions in conventional urea use when they switch to nano urea. In the proposed mechanism, agricultural emission reductions generate credits purchased by regulated industrial emitters, with the value passed to farmers as a discount on nano urea.

It is a clever policy design. The evidentiary base is the weak point. The calculations require credible estimates of actual substitution, nitrogen-use efficiency, emissions avoided, farmer behaviour and crop yields. Those assumptions are doing a great deal of work. Verification at farm level may also be far harder than the article suggests.

I would therefore read this as a proposal worth testing rather than a demonstrated route to reducing India’s fertiliser subsidy or emissions. It would make a good Methods Monday example of how apparently simple policy arithmetic can conceal several counterfactual assumptions.

[Read the Ideas for India analysis](https://www.ideasforindia.in/topics/trade/the-carbon-credit-case-for-nano-urea?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

1.  Wiebe, Keith D., Nicola Cenacchi, Shahnila Dunston, Sherwin Gabriel, Faaiqa Hartley, Abhijeet Mishra, Richard D. Robertson, Mark W. Rosegrant, Timothy B. Sulser, Timothy S. Thomas, and Dirk Willenbockel. 2026. *Global Agrifood Systems Outlook to 2050*. Washington, DC: International Food Policy Research Institute.

Released on 10 September, this is the one global report I would make room for in a South Asia list. IFPRI uses long-run scenario modelling to project changes in production, demand, prices, trade, natural-resource use, food security, nutrition, employment and income through 2050. The report covers seven world regions and seven commodity groups and is explicitly framed as projection rather than prediction.

That distinction matters. Long-horizon models are useful for examining mechanisms and relative trajectories. Their point estimates become increasingly dependent on assumed population, income, climate, productivity and technological pathways as the horizon lengthens.

South Asia is especially interesting here because shifts in diets, labour allocation and agricultural productivity can pull in different directions. IFPRI’s related modelling indicates that healthier-diet scenarios may move low-skilled labour back towards agriculture in parts of South Asia, raising a difficult structural-transformation question rather than producing an uncomplicated welfare story.

## what I would read first

Start with the Bihar TB preprint if you work on implementation or public health. Read the Bangladesh future-of-work report for labour and WEE. Keep the Nepal assessment open beside both: its reconstruction numbers show how quickly climate shocks can alter the development constraints within which every other policy operates.

The nano-urea piece is the one I would argue with most. That is also why it is worth reading.

## one question for readers

I am looking for credible India or South Asia evidence that tests nano urea against conventional urea using farm-level yield, nitrogen-use and emissions outcomes.
