*Good Reads #10 on 24th September, 2026*

*Tamil Nadu’s maternity benefit programme appears to have changed how women use health services. A new study finds something more difficult: those changes did not translate into measurable improvements in children’s nutritional outcomes.*

There is a particularly useful result in a new paper by Shreya Biswas, Upasak Das and Narbadeshwar Mishra.

Tamil Nadu’s Dr. Muthulakshmi Reddy Maternity Benefit Scheme gives pregnant women cash payments tied to behaviours such as registering a pregnancy, delivering in a health facility and completing specified childhood immunisations. The scheme dates to 1987, but the transfers increased substantially from Rs 500 to Rs 6,000 in 2006 and then to Rs 12,000 in 2011. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The authors ask what happened after those increases.

Women did more of many of the things the programme wanted them to do.

Pregnancy registration increased. Institutional deliveries increased. Immunisation increased. Women were also more likely to attend antenatal care, take iron tablets and tetanus injections, and begin breastfeeding immediately after birth.

And yet the researchers find no corresponding improvement in stunting, wasting or underweight among children. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That apparent contradiction is what makes this paper worth reading.

## The difference between doing something and achieving something

Conditional cash transfers have an appealing programme logic.

Identify behaviours associated with better health. Attach a financial incentive to them. Reduce some of the financial constraints facing poorer households. More people use the relevant services. Better health follows.

The first part of that chain appears to have worked rather well in Tamil Nadu.

Using NFHS-3 from 2005-06 and NFHS-4 from 2015-16, the authors estimate that the expansion of MRMBS increased pregnancy registration by 11.9 percentage points and institutional delivery by 10.1 percentage points. Polio and DPT vaccination also increased. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The effects extend beyond the behaviours directly tied to payment.

The estimated probability of receiving at least four antenatal check-ups rose by 10.7 percentage points. Iron-tablet consumption increased by 10 percentage points, tetanus vaccination by 7.2 points and immediate initiation of breastfeeding by 9.9 points. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Those are meaningful behavioural changes.

But the children of eligible women were no less likely to be stunted, wasted or underweight. The authors also find no improvement in the corresponding anthropometric z-scores, anaemia or short-term morbidity. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The programme appears to have moved several intermediate indicators without moving the final indicators the intervention was ultimately intended to improve.

## This is not evidence that the programme failed

That distinction matters.

It would be easy to read the null nutritional results and conclude that the cash transfer did not work. I don’t think the paper supports such a simple conclusion.

Pregnancy registration matters. Antenatal care matters. Immunisation matters. Institutional delivery matters. A programme that increases the use of these services has produced something of value even if anthropometric measures do not subsequently move.

Nor should we expect every maternal-health intervention to produce readily detectable changes in child height and weight.

Stunting is cumulative. Wasting reflects a different nutritional process. Both are produced by many exposures occurring before and after birth. A cash transfer encouraging particular maternal and child health behaviours occupies only one part of that causal chain.

The authors themselves are careful about this. NFHS is a repeated cross-section, not a longitudinal panel. They cannot follow individual children through time. Their estimates therefore capture cohort-level differences associated with programme exposure rather than individual long-term treatment effects. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That makes the absence of an anthropometric effect important, but not a referendum on the entire programme.

## What happened between the clinic and the child?

The authors go looking for mechanisms.

One possibility would be that households received additional cash but spent it in ways unrelated to child health. They find no evidence of increased spending on what the literature rather unfortunately calls “temptation goods,” nor increased possession of durable assets.

There is some evidence of greater access to toilets.

But something else does not change: children’s diets.

The researchers examine consumption across seven food groups and construct a measure of minimum dietary diversity. They find no evidence that children in beneficiary households received better-quality or more diverse diets. Meal frequency did not improve either. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Nor did the programme appear to increase engagement with health workers.

This offers one explanation for the disconnect.

A woman may attend antenatal care because a transfer encourages her to do so. She may deliver in a health facility. Her child may receive the required vaccinations.

None of those changes necessarily means that the child’s diet subsequently becomes more diverse, that the household receives sustained nutritional support, or that interactions with the health system are of sufficient quality to alter longer-term nutritional outcomes.

The transfer can move one part of the chain without moving everything downstream.

## There is another explanation worth considering: Tamil Nadu

The setting makes the paper particularly interesting.

Tamil Nadu was already performing comparatively well on many maternal and child health indicators before the larger transfers were introduced. Among children of eligible mothers in the authors’ pre-expansion data, 28 per cent were stunted and 19 per cent wasted. The state was also improving primary health infrastructure and expanding round-the-clock services at PHCs during this period. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That creates an important evaluation problem.

The effect of introducing an intervention into a poorly functioning health system is not necessarily the same as the effect of adding money to a system in which utilisation is already relatively high.

Marginal gains may become harder to obtain.

The authors recognise this, describing the study partly as an examination of the returns to a cash transfer in a state that had already made substantial progress in maternal and child health. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This also makes me cautious about transporting the result elsewhere.

The finding is not that conditional cash transfers cannot improve child nutrition. It is that this expansion, in this institutional setting, over this period, produced detectable changes in several health behaviours without detectable changes in the nutritional outcomes examined.

That is both narrower and more useful.

## And there are identification questions

The empirical strategy deserves attention too.

The researchers use a triple-difference design, exploiting changes over time, differences between Tamil Nadu and Karnataka, and eligibility according to birth order. NFHS-3 provides the pre-expansion observations and NFHS-4 the post-expansion observations. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is considerably more informative than simply comparing beneficiaries and non-beneficiaries after the programme.

But the design still asks a lot of observational data.

The authors test pre-existing trends using NFHS-2 and NFHS-3. For most outcomes, the relevant coefficients are statistically insignificant. But the assumption does not hold for antenatal-care visits or wasting, and some variables cannot be tested because they were not collected in NFHS-2. The authors explicitly caution against a straightforward causal interpretation of those particular estimates. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

There is another complication. Women in the pre-period were not literally untreated. MRMBS already existed. What changed was the size of the payment, dramatically.

So this is better understood as an evaluation of an expansion in programme generosity than a clean comparison between a world with the programme and one without it.

None of this makes the results uninformative. It tells us how carefully they should be read.

## The indicator ladder

What stayed with me after reading the paper was not really a question about cash transfers.

It was a question about evaluation.

A programme like this contains a sequence:

**eligibility → receipt of cash → use of services → quality of care → changes in household behaviour → improved health**

Each step is an outcome.

But they are not equivalent outcomes.

If institutional delivery increases, we have evidence about institutional delivery. We do not yet have evidence about maternal mortality.

If immunisation increases, we have evidence about immunisation. We do not yet have evidence about nutritional status.

If antenatal visits increase, we know more women reached a health service. We still need to know what happened during those visits.

This sounds obvious when written out. It becomes surprisingly easy to forget once programmes acquire dashboards.

Yesterday’s Research Rundown looked at essentially the same problem in completely different sectors.

***A Bangladeshi MSME can have a digital footprint and use mobile financial services without obtaining formal credit.***

***A worker can register for social protection without necessarily receiving effective protection.***

***A village can acquire network coverage without residents obtaining an affordable, reliable service.***

***And a pregnant woman can reach a health facility without that contact necessarily producing every health outcome farther down the chain.***

***The common mistake is to allow an observable intermediate indicator to stand in for the outcome we actually care about.***

That does not make intermediate indicators unimportant.

It means we should name them correctly.

## What I take from the paper

The policy response to these findings should not be to abandon conditional maternity benefits.

The more interesting response is to ask what additional conditions are required for increased health-service use to become better health.

The authors point towards diet quality and engagement with health workers. The wider literature they discuss suggests others: quality of care, sanitation, public-health infrastructure and the size of the transfer itself. [Taylor & Francis Online](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220388.2026.2727161?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

I would add one more.

Evaluation needs to preserve the distance between stages in the causal chain.

A programme can succeed at one stage and stall at another. Knowing where that happens is more useful than forcing the entire intervention into a binary judgment of success or failure.

This Tamil Nadu study gives us a particularly clean example.

Women changed their behaviour.

The children’s measured nutritional outcomes did not.

The research question begins in the space between those two findings.
