*Research Rundown Methods Monday - 5 October, 2026*

A questionnaire can record an answer accurately and still measure the wrong thing. A change in question order may alter what respondents think they are being asked. A different interview mode may change what they are willing to disclose. A statistical model may produce precise local estimates while making assumptions that are difficult to defend.

*Three recent studies examine how question order changes factual responses, whether video interviews produce comparable data, and how statistical models can improve estimates for small populations.*

## Question order, interview modes and the problem of small-area estimates

Researchers devote considerable attention to sampling, estimation and statistical inference, but some consequential decisions are made much earlier, when questionnaires are designed and data collection procedures are selected. Three papers published in the *Journal of Survey Statistics and Methodology* on 29 September examine different parts of this process. Their findings are particularly relevant to development researchers working with repeated surveys, mixed-mode data collection and small geographical populations.

The first paper demonstrates how the order of two apparently straightforward questions can influence factual responses. The second tests the measurement consequences of replacing face-to-face interviews with video calls. The third addresses a different problem: obtaining reliable estimates for geographical areas where survey samples are too small to support precise direct estimation.

Together, they provide useful evidence about decisions researchers often make before considering their implications for measurement quality.

## 1. Even factual questions are sensitive to question order.

Daniel Goldstein's paper, *Room for Error: Identifying Question Order Effects in Factual Measures When Meaning or Context Shifts*, examines how respondents interpret questions about the number of rooms and bedrooms in their homes.

Using data from three surveys conducted in New York City, Goldstein investigates differences in reported housing characteristics when respondents encounter these questions in different sequences. In surveys where respondents were asked about total rooms before bedrooms, interview records indicated greater confusion. When the bedroom question appeared first, or the two questions were presented closer together, respondents tended to report a larger number of total rooms.

Interviewer feedback suggested a possible explanation. Some respondents interpreted a question about rooms as asking about bedrooms, apparently because bedroom counts were a more familiar way of describing housing size. Asking about bedrooms first helped establish the distinction between the two concepts.

The finding matters because the questions concern characteristics that might ordinarily be regarded as objective. Housing size does not change when a questionnaire changes. Its reported value can.

There are implications for longitudinal surveys, particularly where researchers revise instruments between waves. A change in reported housing characteristics may reflect a change in the measurement process rather than an actual change in living conditions. Researchers examining time trends therefore need to consider whether modifications to question order have introduced discontinuities in the resulting series.

The relevance extends to Indian household surveys. Questions about household composition, employment, dwelling characteristics and access to public services frequently require respondents to distinguish between related concepts. The appropriate sequence may depend on which categories respondents already understand and use in everyday conversation.

Goldstein's evidence comes from comparisons across survey implementations and associated interview records. The study provides a persuasive account of how question order can influence comprehension, although other differences between survey implementations must be considered when interpreting the magnitude of the effects.

I would recommend this paper to researchers designing panel questionnaires or revising established instruments. It is a useful reminder that preserving questionnaire wording alone does not guarantee measurement comparability.

## 2. Video interviews produce results similar to face-to-face interviews, with some qualifications.

Marc Asensio, Matt Brown, Richard Silverwood, Tim Hanson and Gabriele Durrant examine whether video interviewing offers a credible alternative to conventional face-to-face data collection.

Their study, *Assessing the Impact of Video Interviewing on Measurement Quality: Evidence from an Experimental Study on Mode Effects*, randomly assigned 1,692 respondents aged between 20 and 40 to video, web or face-to-face interviewing. Participants came from a nonprobability convenience sample.

The authors examine two dimensions of measurement quality: item nonresponse and the distribution of answers across interview modes.

Video interviewing produced slightly lower item nonresponse than face-to-face interviewing, although the differences were very small. Responses obtained through the two interviewer-administered modes were also broadly similar.

The more substantial differences appeared between video interviews and self-administered web surveys. These were particularly apparent for questions concerning sensitive subjects, including mental wellbeing and financial difficulties.

This distinction deserves attention. Video interviewing retains the presence of an interviewer, even when that interaction occurs remotely. Respondents may therefore experience some of the same pressures to provide socially acceptable answers that arise during face-to-face interviews.

The experiment supports the possibility of using video interviewing as an alternative mode for some types of surveys. However, its design places limits on how widely the findings can be applied. Random allocation identifies mode differences among the recruited participants, while the convenience sample restricts claims about the wider population. The experiment also examines a relatively narrow age group.

For development research in India, the practical implications depend on the population being surveyed. Video interviewing requires access to suitable devices, stable connectivity and sufficient digital familiarity. These requirements can introduce coverage and participation problems that a measurement experiment among digitally accessible respondents may fail to capture.

There are also questions about interview privacy. A respondent answering questions about household finances, gender relations or personal experiences may have less control over who can hear the conversation during a video call.

The paper is particularly useful for research teams planning mixed-mode surveys. It suggests that video and face-to-face interviews can generate comparable responses under some conditions. However, researchers would still need to assess selection into interview modes, item sensitivity and the conditions under which respondents participate.

The distinction between measurement quality and population coverage is particularly important when considering whether remote interviewing can replace household visits.

Asensio, Marc, Matt Brown, Richard Silverwood, Tim Hanson, and Gabriele Durrant. 2026. “Assessing the Impact of Video Interviewing on Measurement Quality: Evidence from an Experimental Study on Mode Effects.” *Journal of Survey Statistics and Methodology*, published 29 September 2026.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/jssam/smag024).

## 3. Small-area estimation models need to account for differences between areas.

Many development surveys are designed to produce estimates at national or state level, although policymakers frequently require information for districts, blocks or smaller administrative units. Direct estimates for these populations can be unreliable when the number of observations is small.

Small-area estimation addresses this problem by combining survey information with auxiliary variables through statistical models. A common approach uses area-level models that assume a shared relationship between the outcome and its predictors across geographical areas.

Xin Wang and Konnor Payne examine what happens when this assumption is too restrictive.

Their paper, *Model-Based Estimators Using Area-Level Models With Clustered Effects*, proposes an estimator that allows regression coefficients to differ across groups of areas. Rather than requiring researchers to specify these groups in advance, the method identifies clusters through a penalised estimation procedure.

The basic idea is that different geographical areas may share similar relationships between an outcome and its predictors without all areas following the same model.

The authors evaluate the estimator through simulation studies and an application involving forest inventory data from 168 counties in the western United States. Their empirical example estimates mean tree carbon per acre using forest inventory measurements and remotely sensed information about vegetation.

The proposed method identifies four groups of counties with different regression coefficients. In the application, allowing these differences reduces the coefficient of variation of estimates relative to conventional models with common regression slopes.

The method has potential applications in development statistics. Relationships between household characteristics and poverty, nutrition, employment or service access may vary across districts. An auxiliary variable that predicts an outcome well in one group of districts may perform differently elsewhere.

This is particularly relevant when administrative records, census variables or geospatial indicators are used to generate estimates below the geographical level supported by a survey's original design.

There are limitations. The estimator assumes that areas can be grouped according to shared regression coefficients, and its performance depends on how well that structure represents the data. The authors acknowledge computational difficulties when the number of areas becomes large. Their framework also does not directly accommodate areas without observed survey estimates unless further information is available.

The practical test for Indian applications would involve comparing the method with existing small-area estimators using survey and auxiliary datasets where direct estimates are sufficiently reliable for validation. Model-based precision alone would provide an incomplete assessment, particularly where auxiliary data quality differs systematically across geographical areas.

I would recommend this paper to researchers working with district-level indicators, poverty mapping or the integration of survey and administrative data. It offers a useful methodological extension, although applications to development indicators will need empirical validation.

Wang, Xin, and Konnor Payne. 2026. “Model-Based Estimators Using Area-Level Models With Clustered Effects.” *Journal of Survey Statistics and Methodology*, published 29 September 2026.

[Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/jssam/smag033).

## What I would take from these papers

Goldstein's paper is the most immediately useful for researchers developing household survey instruments. It identifies a measurement problem that can arise even when questions appear unambiguous to the people who designed them. The implications for questionnaire pretesting and longitudinal comparability are direct.

The video-interviewing experiment provides evidence for decisions about survey administration, particularly when fieldwork costs or access constraints make conventional interviewing difficult. Its findings should be considered alongside evidence on coverage, participation and respondent privacy.

Wang and Payne's contribution is more technical. Their estimator addresses heterogeneity across small areas, which is relevant to the increasing demand for district-level and subdistrict-level statistics. Its practical value will depend on the availability and quality of auxiliary information and on whether the assumed clusters provide a credible description of the populations being studied.

I would begin with Goldstein's paper because questionnaire sequencing is a design decision that nearly every survey researcher encounters. It also offers a fairly inexpensive opportunity for empirical testing. A small randomised experiment during piloting could establish whether alternative question sequences materially change responses before the final instrument enters the field.
