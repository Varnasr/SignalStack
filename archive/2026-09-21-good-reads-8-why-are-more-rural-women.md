*Good Reads #8 on 20th September, 2026*

One of the largest changes in India’s labour market over the past decade has happened among rural women.

The rural female workforce participation rate for women aged 15 and above rose from 23.7 per cent in 2017-18 to 44.8 per cent in 2025. In absolute terms, Bishwanath Goldar and Suresh Chand Aggarwal estimate that rural female employment increased from about 75 million women to 150 million over the same period. Much of this increase occurred in agriculture and allied work, including a substantial increase in women classified as unpaid family workers.

The numbers are striking. Their interpretation is much harder.

In a recent Ideas for India essay, Goldar and Aggarwal challenge an increasingly common explanation: that women have entered rural employment primarily because household economic conditions have deteriorated.

Their argument is based on a longer working paper published in July 2026.

## the argument

The distress explanation has considerable intuitive appeal.

If household income comes under pressure, women who were previously outside measured employment may enter paid work, agriculture or family enterprises. Rising participation can therefore coexist with worsening household circumstances. Several recent analyses have pointed to precisely this possibility, particularly because much of the increase in women’s work has occurred in agriculture, self-employment and unpaid family work.

Goldar and Aggarwal ask whether the available evidence supports such a broad conclusion.

One of their more interesting tests looks at consumption groups.

If economic distress were the dominant explanation, we might expect the increase in women’s employment to be concentrated among poorer households. Their comparison of PLFS data finds increases across the consumption distribution. Rural female workforce participation increased by an average of 17.8 percentage points among the bottom three consumption deciles and by 13 percentage points among the top three.

Women from relatively better-off farming households also account for a meaningful share of the increase in unpaid agricultural work.

This creates a problem for a simple distress story.

The authors also point towards alternative mechanisms. These include expansion of women’s collectives and credit through DAY-NRLM, employment through MGNREGS, growth in agriculture and allied activities, and reductions in the time required for household tasks as access to water, sanitation and clean cooking fuel has expanded.

Amarjeet Sinha has made a related argument, attributing part of the rise in rural women’s participation to MGNREGS, DAY-NRLM and greater recognition of women’s agricultural activity. His analysis also concedes something worth keeping in view: increased participation through these routes need not produce large increases in women’s incomes.

## where I am persuaded

Goldar and Aggarwal are right to challenge the tendency to turn one plausible mechanism into a complete explanation.

The consumption-decile result deserves attention. A phenomenon occurring across the income distribution requires a wider explanation than one driven exclusively by severe household distress.

Their paper also reminds us that agricultural work classified as “unpaid” cannot automatically be treated as economically meaningless. Family farming combines labour, household production, land, livestock and market activity in ways that conventional employment categories handle rather badly.

The broader point is methodological. An observed increase in labour supply does not identify the reason for that increase.

We need evidence on mechanisms.

## where I remain unconvinced

I think the authors push their counterargument further than the evidence allows.

Finding increased participation among higher consumption deciles weakens a pure distress hypothesis. It does not settle whether deteriorating economic conditions contributed substantially to the overall increase.

Consumption deciles are themselves outcomes of household income, production, transfers, prices and labour supply. Households higher in the consumption distribution can experience income shocks. Agricultural households can respond to changing labour markets by reallocating women’s work within family enterprises. A comparison across deciles therefore gives us useful descriptive evidence, while causal interpretation remains difficult.

The composition of the employment increase also matters enormously.

A rise in women’s work has a different welfare meaning when it comes through regular paid employment, profitable own-account activity, MGNREGS employment, or unpaid work on a household farm.

Singh and Ankita’s competing analysis points directly to this issue. They find much of the increase occurring among unpaid and subsidiary workers and report stagnant or declining real earnings over the period they study. They interpret these patterns as evidence consistent with distress-driven employment.

These accounts should therefore be read together.

The disagreement is analytically useful because both sides are trying to infer household behaviour from labour-market outcomes that were never designed to identify motivation.

## the measurement problem

This is the part I found most interesting.

“Why did women start working?” sounds like a straightforward empirical question. PLFS can tell us whether somebody worked, her employment status, industry, occupation and several other characteristics.

It cannot directly tell us why she entered work.

Distress is a latent construct here. So are improved opportunity, changing social norms, reduced domestic work burdens and increased recognition of work women were already doing.

Researchers have to infer these mechanisms from observed patterns.

That means evidence which discriminates between explanations becomes especially valuable: longitudinal household income and employment data, changes in women’s earnings, transitions between employment categories, hours worked, time-use data, local labour demand, programme exposure, and women’s own accounts of why their work changed.

Without those pieces, several explanations can fit the same headline statistic.

## why read it?

Because the rise in rural women’s employment is too large to treat as a statistical curiosity, and too consequential to explain with a single convenient story.

Goldar and Aggarwal provide a useful challenge to the distress interpretation. I am less persuaded by their stronger suggestion that the evidence substantially displaces it.

The more interesting conclusion is that we still do not understand the shift well enough.

That is a good reason to read the piece.

And a good reason to keep asking what exactly a rising female workforce participation rate is measuring.
