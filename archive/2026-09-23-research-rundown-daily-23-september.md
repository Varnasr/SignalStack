## FIVE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

### 1. ADB now sees El Niño joining the energy shock as South Asia’s next macroeconomic risk

The Asian Development Bank’s September outlook, released today, raises its 2026 growth forecast for developing Asia and the Pacific slightly to **5.0 per cent**, from 4.9 per cent in July. That small upgrade should not obscure the change in the risk assessment. ADB now expects a strengthening El Niño to threaten harvests and hydropower alongside the continuing energy shock. ([Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/publications/asian-development-outlook-september-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This matters particularly for South Asia because the two shocks can interact. Higher energy costs raise irrigation, fertiliser, transport and electricity costs. Drier conditions can reduce agricultural output and hydropower generation, increasing demand for expensive imported energy.

The point is not the 0.1 percentage-point forecast revision. Forecast changes of that size are well inside the uncertainty surrounding regional projections. The important development is the **composition of risk**: food, electricity and household purchasing power can now be affected through several channels at once.

[Asian Development Outlook, September 2026](https://www.adb.org/publications/asian-development-outlook-september-2026?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 2. Bangladesh’s growth forecast has been cut again

ADB now expects Bangladesh’s economy to grow by **4.0 per cent in FY2027**, down from the 4.5 per cent forecast in July. The earlier forecast had already been reduced because of weak exports, subdued private investment, energy constraints and banking-sector problems. ([Jagonews24](https://www.jagonews24.com/en/biz-econ/news/96351?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The revision matters because Bangladesh is being hit through several channels simultaneously. The energy disruption raises production costs and constrains factories. Inflation remains high. Problems in the banking system restrict investment, while external demand remains uncertain.

This is also a useful reminder about forecasts. A downward revision is new information about an institution’s assessment of the economy. It is not an observed fall in GDP. The 4.0 per cent figure is conditional on assumptions about energy prices, external demand, domestic policy and the duration of current disruptions.

For Bangladesh, the next few months of industrial production, exports and private credit will tell us more than the forecast itself.

### 3. Women-led MSMEs in Bangladesh are digitally active and still struggle to obtain formal credit

A study released in Dhaka surveyed **329 MSMEs across all eight administrative divisions**, supplemented by 19 key-informant interviews, 12 in-depth interviews and four focus groups. It found that 87 per cent of surveyed businesses had some digital footprint and 97 per cent used mobile financial services, yet only **11.55 per cent had accessed digitally enabled credit**. ([bdnews24.com](https://bdnews24.com/business/htbuhps4ue?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Women-led businesses were especially interesting. Women entrepreneurs used an average of 2.75 digital platforms, compared with 1.88 among male-led enterprises, and 83 per cent expressed interest in digital credit. Yet women-led businesses receive less than 10 per cent of formal SME credit, according to the study. ([The Daily Star](https://www.thedailystar.net/business/news/women-led-msmes-lead-digital-finance-still-face-heavy-loan-barriers-study-4280846?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The survey is too small to support precise national prevalence estimates, and the sampling design matters before treating its percentages as representative of all Bangladeshi MSMEs. The mechanism it identifies is plausible and important: lenders still rely heavily on collateral and documentation even when digital transactions provide other information about a firm.

Digital inclusion and credit inclusion are therefore different outcomes.

### 4. India’s states face a fiscal problem that national public-finance aggregates can hide

A background paper prepared by two RBI researchers for the Union Finance Ministry’s meeting with state finance ministers argues that demographic ageing, urbanisation, climate change and technological change will place increasing pressure on state budgets. States account for roughly **two-thirds of India’s general-government expenditure**. ([The Financial Express](https://www.financialexpress.com/policy/economy-centre-flags-new-fiscal-strains-for-states-from-ageing-ai-climate-risks-4344915/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Ageing raises pension and health expenditure. Climate adaptation requires investment before losses occur. Urbanisation creates infrastructure demands. At the same time, states retain substantial debt burdens and limited room to expand some own-source revenues.

This is worth reading alongside debates about India’s aggregate fiscal position. Many of the services through which citizens experience the state, including health, education, water, transport and substantial parts of social protection, depend heavily on state finances.

The paper estimates that India would need annual real GDP growth of about 8 per cent to reach the government’s 2047 development objective. That is a scenario requirement, rather than a forecast. ([The Financial Express](https://www.financialexpress.com/policy/economy-centre-flags-new-fiscal-strains-for-states-from-ageing-ai-climate-risks-4344915/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### 5. More than 23,000 informal workers across India, Nepal and Pakistan gained access to formalisation and social-protection pathways through two ILO programmes

The International Labour Organization has published results from two recently completed South Asian programmes. It reports that more than **23,000 informal workers** in India, Nepal and Pakistan obtained improved access to formal employment, social protection, representation or related services. A parallel programme worked on child-labour institutions in India and Nepal. ([International Labour Organization](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/japan-supported-ilo-initiatives-strengthen-protections-workers-and-children?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The wording matters. “Improved access” combines several possible outcomes and should not be read as 23,000 workers entering permanent formal employment. Organisation, registration, social-protection enrolment and actual receipt of benefits are distinct stages.

That distinction is particularly important in South Asia, where formalisation is often measured administratively. Registration is relatively easy to count. Whether registration produces insurance coverage, income security, bargaining power or more stable employment requires follow-up data.

[ILO: worker formalisation and child-labour programmes in South Asia](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/japan-supported-ilo-initiatives-strengthen-protections-workers-and-children?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

# ONE PAPER WORTH READING

### The health production function in South Asia

Mohammad Ridwan and colleagues assemble annual data for Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan and Sri Lanka from **2000 to 2025** and examine associations between life expectancy and education, health expenditure, GDP per capita, government effectiveness and urbanisation. Their principal estimates use panel ARDL models, with Driscoll-Kraay errors, PCSE, FGLS, System GMM and bootstrap causality tests as additional specifications. Education and GDP per capita are positively associated with longevity; the health-expenditure coefficient is positive but sensitive to model choice. ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2026.1866937/full?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The paper is useful partly because its difficulties are instructive. Seven countries over 26 years provide a small macro panel, and national averages hide large within-country differences. More importantly, cointegration, Granger causality and System GMM do not by themselves establish causal effects. Government effectiveness and urbanisation even acquire negative coefficients in some specifications, a warning against attaching simple structural interpretations to the estimates.

I would read this as evidence about **macro-level associations**, especially the consistent education relationship, rather than as estimates of what an additional dollar of health spending will do to life expectancy.

**Citation:** Ridwan, M., Z. A. Antor, J. Ko, M. E. Hossain, C. K. Leung, W-K. Ming, and M. Z. Rehman. 2026. “The Health Production Function in South Asia: Panel Evidence on Education, Health Expenditure, Governance, and Longevity.” *Frontiers in Public Health* 14:1866937. # ONE NUMBER

## 11.55 per cent

The share of the 329 Bangladeshi MSMEs surveyed that had accessed digitally enabled credit, despite **87 per cent having a digital footprint and 97 per cent using mobile financial services**. ([bdnews24.com](https://bdnews24.com/business/htbuhps4ue?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This is a useful distinction between infrastructure and outcome.

A firm can have a mobile wallet, digital transaction history and online presence while remaining outside formal credit markets. Digital adoption therefore cannot serve as a proxy for financial inclusion without specifying which financial service is actually being measured.

The sample is small enough that I would resist treating 11.55 per cent as a national estimate. The gap between digital use and digital borrowing is the more defensible finding.

# POLICY WATCH

## Pakistan is putting Rs33 billion into connecting places the market has left behind

Pakistan’s Universal Service Fund policy committee has approved a **Rs33 billion budget** for expanding digital connectivity in unserved and underserved areas. The programme is explicitly intended to address locations where commercial provision remains weak. ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/latest-news/2026-09-23?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This sits neatly beside today’s Bangladesh evidence because it raises the next question in digital-inclusion policy.

Coverage is the first constraint. Usage is another. Product suitability, affordability and access to credit or other economically useful services come later.

The evaluation sequence should therefore resist treating network expansion as equivalent to economic inclusion. Useful indicators would move from population covered, to actual subscriptions and regular use, to service quality and price, and eventually to outcomes such as access to markets, finance or employment.

The distinction sounds obvious. It is frequently lost once programmes begin reporting kilometres of fibre, towers installed or people nominally covered.

# EDITORIAL NOTE

## Access is a chain, and we keep measuring its first link

Three items today share a measurement problem.

Pakistan can extend a telecommunications network. Bangladesh can have almost universal mobile-finance use among a sample of MSMEs. An ILO programme can help an informal worker register for social protection.

All are meaningful changes. None tells us whether the final economic outcome occurred.

Development programmes frequently have a chain running from **availability → eligibility → enrolment → use → effective service → outcome**. Administrative systems become progressively weaker as we move along it.

That creates a systematic bias in what governments and programmes know about themselves. Inputs and registrations are observed quickly and cheaply. Failed transactions, abandoned applications, poor service quality and people who remain eligible but never enrol are harder to see.

The Bangladesh MSME result is a particularly clean example. A digital footprint exists. Mobile financial services are being used. Credit still does not follow.

For evaluation, I would increasingly ask programmes to specify **where in the access chain their headline indicator sits** before accepting it as evidence of inclusion.

**Reader question:** What is the strongest study you know that follows the same people or firms through several stages of an access chain, from eligibility and enrolment through actual use to an economic or welfare outcome?

This one belongs in a future Methods Monday. There is a useful piece to write on why coverage, take-up, utilisation and effective access should be treated as separate estimands.
