Floods expose the distance between climate risk and preparedness, a Pakistan study asks what happens when women lose cash transfers, and new work puts numbers on food lost before it reaches the market.

## FIVE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

### 1. Northern India and Nepal are dealing with another severe flood shock

Uttar Pradesh received 66.5 mm of rain between 25 and 27 September, against a normal 7.6 mm for those dates. By 29 September, 56 deaths had been reported in India. Nepal reported 14 deaths and 11 people missing, while authorities warned of possible flash floods in 49 of its 77 districts.

For Nepal, the timing matters. These floods arrived only a month after the late-August disaster that killed at least 1,450 people. Disaster risk is therefore becoming a question of repeated shocks and recovery capacity, rather than isolated emergencies.

There is a measurement problem here too. Rainfall, mortality and physical damage are reported quickly. Lost work, debt, disrupted schooling, migration and the distribution of recovery spending emerge much later, if they are measured at all. Those are the outcomes that will tell us whether repeated climate shocks are changing household trajectories.

### 2. A new Pakistan paper examines what happens when cash transfers suddenly stop

A new World Bank Policy Research Working Paper by Nasir Iqbal, Amen Jalal, Mahreen Mahmud and Kate Vyborny studies an unusually neglected part of social protection: exit.

Female-targeted cash transfers are commonly assessed by comparing recipients with non-recipients. This paper instead asks what happens when women unexpectedly cease to qualify. The authors use a regression discontinuity-in-differences design around a change in programme eligibility in Pakistan.

That distinction matters. A transfer can alter bargaining arrangements inside a household while it is being paid. Whether those changes survive after the money disappears is a separate empirical question. Programme evaluations that stop at the final payment can therefore miss part of the effect.

More on the paper below.

### 3. India may be measuring post-harvest loss at the wrong level of aggregation

A new WRI India working paper examines soyabean, custard apple and onion supply chains in Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra. Its useful contribution is fairly practical: losses occur at different points for different crops.

For soyabean, harvesting, threshing and collection emerge as critical points. For onion, farm-level storage dominates. Custard apple losses vary according to whether production comes from forests and common land, farm boundaries or orchards.

This makes a single category called “post-harvest loss” analytically weak for programme design. An intervention aimed at storage may make sense for onions and miss much of the problem elsewhere. The study uses mixed methods and maps actors and loss points across the selected supply chains. Its geographic and crop coverage is deliberately narrow, so the estimates should not be read as national loss rates.

### 4. South Asia’s innovation rankings improved, though rankings need careful reading

WIPO’s 2026 Global Innovation Index places India 38th globally. Pakistan rises four places to 95th, Nepal reaches 104th and Bangladesh 105th. Bhutan enters the index at 74th. India remains the highest-ranked economy in Central and Southern Asia.

The more interesting finding sits below the league table. India ranks 49th on innovation inputs and 25th on outputs. WIPO classifies India as an innovation “overperformer” relative to its income level for the sixteenth consecutive year. Pakistan also returns to that group, with particular strengths in mobile-app creation and ICT-service exports.

Composite indices remain sensitive to indicator choice, missing data, normalisation and weighting. Movement of a few ranks should therefore carry less weight than changes in the underlying indicators.

### 5. A sovereign rating may tell us less about fiscal fundamentals than its precision suggests

A paper by Olivier Blanchard, Daniel Leigh and Prachi Mishra examines how sovereign credit ratings relate to debt and projected primary balances.

Their model-based comparison finds three discrepancies. Ratings place considerably more weight on debt than on forecast primary balances, give too little weight to the difference between interest and growth rates, and contain large country effects. Economies with similar debt and fiscal trajectories can consequently receive very different ratings.

For developing countries, this is more than an argument about rating methodology. Sovereign ratings affect borrowing costs and perceptions of fiscal space. If persistent country effects partly reflect factors outside the basic debt arithmetic, the question becomes what those effects are measuring.

The paper is an IMF working paper. It should be read as research in progress rather than an institutional IMF position.

## ONE PAPER WORTH READING

### What Happens When Women Suddenly Stop Receiving Cash Transfers?

Nasir Iqbal, Amen Jalal, Mahreen Mahmud and Kate Vyborny, World Bank Policy Research Working Paper 11474, 29 September 2026.

Most cash-transfer research asks what happens when money arrives. This paper asks the reverse question.

The setting is Pakistan. The authors exploit a revised eligibility rule that caused some women to lose transfers unexpectedly and use a regression discontinuity-in-differences design around the eligibility threshold. That gives the paper a stronger identification strategy than a simple comparison of former recipients with continuing recipients, since those groups could differ systematically for many other reasons.

The substantive question is unusually useful: does the position women acquire while bringing programme income into the household persist when that income disappears, and is sudden programme exit associated with backlash?

The design still identifies effects around an eligibility threshold. External validity therefore needs care. Women farther from that threshold, households leaving programmes for other reasons and planned graduation may behave differently.

The larger contribution is conceptual. Social-protection evaluations routinely devote considerable attention to targeting and programme entry. Exit receives much less scrutiny. If transfers alter bargaining power, labour decisions or household expectations, the end of a programme is itself an intervention. It should be measured as one.

## ONE NUMBER

### 66.5 mm

Rain recorded in Uttar Pradesh between 25 and 27 September.

The normal rainfall for the same period is 7.6 mm. In other words, the state received almost nine times the normal amount for those dates.

A rainfall anomaly does not by itself establish the contribution of anthropogenic climate change to a particular flood. Attribution requires a separate analysis. It does tell us the scale of the immediate hydrological shock confronting infrastructure, agriculture and local disaster-response systems.

## POLICY WATCH

### Food loss policy needs crop-specific evidence

India has spent years discussing cold chains, storage and food loss as broad infrastructure problems. The new WRI India study offers a useful corrective.

Different crops are being lost at different stages. For onion, storage is central. For soyabean, losses arise earlier in harvesting, threshing and collection. Custard apple presents another structure again.

That changes the policy question. Aggregate estimates can establish the size of a problem. They are a poor guide to where public money should be spent unless they identify where loss occurs, who bears it and why.

The next useful evidence would connect physical quantities lost at each stage to household income, prices, nutrition and emissions. Without that link, tonnes lost remain an incomplete welfare measure.

## EDITORIAL NOTE

There is a common measurement problem running through today’s material.

Flood losses are easiest to count when they involve rainfall, roads and deaths. Food loss becomes one national percentage even when crops disappear at different points in the supply chain. Innovation becomes a rank. Social protection success becomes the effect observed while payments continue.

Aggregation is necessary. It also removes information.

For research intended to guide policy, the relevant question is often one level below the headline indicator: where did the loss occur, who experienced it, when did the effect appear, and did it persist?

That is where several of today’s stories become more interesting.

### A question for readers

If a cash transfer changes women’s bargaining position while payments continue, how long should an evaluation follow households after programme exit before we can reasonably say whether that change persisted? I would be particularly interested in comparable evidence from South Asia on programme graduation, discontinuation or payment shocks.

This is also a measurement question worth returning to in a future Methods Monday: how should evaluations define the treatment period when an intervention may continue to affect behaviour after the intervention itself ends?
