## Five things worth knowing

### 1. Bangladesh is trying housing finance without the mortgage

The Asian Development Bank approved a **$100 million concessional loan** for affordable and climate-resilient housing in Bangladesh. The interesting part is the financing model.

The money will flow through Palli Karma-Sahayak Foundation and eligible microfinance institutions, providing long-term, collateral-free housing loans to low- and middle-income households. Conventional mortgage markets often exclude households with informal or irregular incomes, even when those households can service smaller loans. [Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/adb-approves-100-million-loan-expand-access-affordable-resilient-housing-finance-bangladesh)

ADB expects the programme to reach at least **40,000 households by 2031**, including at least 32,000 women and 24,000 borrowers living in areas exposed to natural hazards. Forty million dollars of the financing is classified as climate adaptation finance. [Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/adb-approves-100-million-loan-expand-access-affordable-resilient-housing-finance-bangladesh)

There are at least three interventions bundled together here: access to credit, women’s housing security and climate-resilient construction. That makes the eventual evaluation interesting. Loan disbursement will be the easiest outcome to count. Whether households gain secure tenure, safer housing and greater resilience after a shock will take longer to establish.

[ADB: Inclusive Affordable Housing Finance Project](https://www.adb.org/news/adb-approves-100-million-loan-expand-access-affordable-resilient-housing-finance-bangladesh?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 2. Bangladesh is also paying out considerably more foreign debt than it is receiving

There is another financing story in Bangladesh today.

Economic Relations Division data reported by *The Business Standard* show that Bangladesh received **$294.56 million** in foreign loans and grants during July and August of FY2026–27, while paying **$698.92 million** in foreign debt service.

Of that repayment, $516.04 million was principal and $182.88 million interest. Foreign assistance disbursements during the same two months of the previous financial year were $750 million. [The Business Standard](https://www.tbsnews.net/economy/bangladesh-pays-699m-foreign-debt-receives-295m-july-august-1554971)

Two months do not establish an annual trend, and disbursement schedules can be lumpy. But the comparison is still useful. Gross development-finance commitments tell us little about the net resource flow unless repayments are placed beside them.

### 3. Child development in Pakistan does not follow a single provincial pattern

A new study uses data on **46,067 children aged 36–59 months** across four Pakistani provinces to examine developmental attainment.

Its most useful finding may be the absence of a simple provincial hierarchy. Performance varies across developmental domains, while socioeconomic gradients appear in every province. Early-childhood education attendance is associated with higher developmental attainment; stunting and functional difficulties are associated with poorer attainment. The relationship with adult stimulation differs across settings. [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9067/13/10/1314?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This matters for how we use composite early-childhood indicators. A single national or provincial score can conceal different deficits in literacy-numeracy, physical development, learning and socio-emotional development.

It also argues against treating “early childhood development” as one intervention problem. Nutrition, disability, preschool participation, household resources and caregiving environments do not operate through identical pathways.

[Read the study in Children](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9067/13/10/1314?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 4. Bangladesh’s manufacturing problem is not simply that it imports too much

A new open-access *Scientific Reports* paper examines Bangladesh’s electrical-equipment manufacturing sector through trade dependence, domestic industrial capacity and competitiveness.

The sector is an interesting case for industrial policy. Expanding electricity systems and urbanisation create domestic demand for electrical equipment, but high demand does not by itself create domestic manufacturing capability. The paper examines the relationship between import dependence and the constraints facing local production rather than treating import substitution as a slogan. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-72639-z?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The distinction matters. Reducing imports can be an objective on paper. Building firms capable of producing reliable goods at competitive prices requires technology, capital, standards, skills, supply chains and sufficient market scale.

For development policy, the better question is therefore not simply *what does Bangladesh import?* It is *which capabilities prevent domestic firms from producing it competitively?*

### 5. Climate migration policy in Bangladesh is moving beyond the place people leave

A workshop organised by the Refugee and Migratory Movements Research Unit in Chattogram has proposed a five-part coordination model involving government, business, civil society, media and local communities to support migrants arriving in the city because of climate and other pressures. [The Business Standard](https://www.tbsnews.net/bangladesh/migration/pentagonal-model-proposed-coordinate-jobs-services-ctg-migrants-1555286?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The proposal itself is preliminary. The more interesting shift is conceptual.

Climate-migration policy is often organised around vulnerable places of origin: eroding coastlines, flood-prone settlements or areas exposed to salinity and extreme weather. But adaptation through migration also creates policy obligations at the destination.

Where do people live? How do they enter urban labour markets? Can children enter school? Can families obtain health services and social protection? What happens when documentation remains tied to another place?

If migration is an adaptation strategy, receiving cities are part of climate-adaptation policy too.

## Paper of the day

### What happened when India subsidised LPG connections for women?

Bharti Nandwani and Manisha Jain have a new paper in the *Journal of Environmental Management* examining the Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana, which provided subsidised LPG connections to women from economically and socially disadvantaged households.

The paper was published online on 26 September. Its question is larger than fuel adoption: what effect did a gender-targeted clean-energy policy have on women’s well-being? [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42800272/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

PMUY is especially useful for evaluation because it sits at the intersection of energy poverty, household air pollution and gender. Women disproportionately undertake cooking, so the time and health costs associated with solid fuels are also unequally distributed within households.

But an LPG **connection** is not the same thing as sustained LPG **use**. Refill affordability, fuel prices, household preferences and access to distributors can all affect what happens after a connection is issued.

That distinction is central to evaluating clean-cooking programmes. Administrative data can tell us how many connections exist. The harder outcomes are fuel stacking, frequency of LPG use, exposure to smoke, time use and changes in women’s welfare.

[Nandwani and Jain, Journal of Environmental Management](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42800272/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## Policy watch

### Housing is becoming part of climate finance

The Bangladesh housing project is worth watching for a reason beyond its $100 million size.

ADB has designated **$40 million** of its financing as climate adaptation finance, while the housing loans are intended to support construction capable of better withstanding floods, cyclones and extreme heat. [Asian Development Bank](https://www.adb.org/news/adb-approves-100-million-loan-expand-access-affordable-resilient-housing-finance-bangladesh)

This broadens what adaptation spending looks like.

Climate resilience is often discussed through embankments, drainage, early-warning systems or large infrastructure. But for a household exposed to repeated flooding or extreme heat, the design and location of the house itself may be adaptation infrastructure.

The evaluation question will be whether financing produces homes that are measurably safer when hazards occur, rather than simply financing more construction.

## A note from me

There is an interesting thread running through today’s material.

A housing loan is not secure housing.

An LPG connection is not clean cooking.

Foreign assistance received is not the same as the net flow after debt repayment.

Migration away from a climate-exposed place is not successful adaptation if the destination offers insecure housing, poor work and weak access to services.

And an early-childhood-development score can hide very different developmental problems.

These are all reminders to ask what sits between an intervention and the outcome attached to it.

We often measure the first observable change because it arrives quickly and sits neatly in administrative data. Connections. Loans. Enrolments. Disbursements. Migration counts.

The harder work starts one step later.

**What happened after access was created?**

That is usually where the development question becomes more interesting.
