## When the numbers get better

*Employment is up. Poverty is down. Growth has held up. Some rural wages are falling. Food inflation is rising. The problem is not deciding which number is correct. It is deciding what each number allows us to say about how people are living.*

### From the editor

*If you have been reading Research Rundown for a while, this issue may feel a little different.*

When I started the Rundown in 2024, it was a monthly collection of things I thought were worth reading, watching, questioning or trying. Papers sat beside podcasts, research methods, books, datasets, jobs and the occasional bit of research humour. I still like that version of it.

But Research Rundown has acquired several younger siblings since then. The Daily follows research and policy as they appear. Friday Findings does the heavier paper-finding. Methods Monday gives me somewhere to obsess over measurement and research design. Good Reads gives me room for work that deserves attention without requiring an entire issue.

That leaves the original Rundown free to do something I have increasingly wanted it to do: stay with a question for longer.

From this issue, the flagship Research Rundown will appear every two months. Each edition will begin with one problem and stay with it for a while: what the evidence says, where I think it is persuasive, where I think we are asking too much of it, and what I am still not sure about.

There will still be papers, methods, research practice, things worth reading and the occasional detour. I am keeping the magazine, but giving it more charecter. Ideas will sometimes travel. Something first spotted in a Daily may return here after more evidence appears. A measurement problem from Methods Monday may change how we read a policy claim. A paper from Friday Findings may eventually become part of a larger argument.

For the first issue in this form, I want to start with a problem that has been following me through several recent releases.

**India’s economic indicators have been producing quite a lot of good news. I am increasingly interested in what happens when we put the good news next to one another, and then ask what exactly has improved.**

## The question

On 15 September, India’s latest labour-market numbers arrived.

They were good.

The labour-force participation rate among people aged 15 and above reached 55.6 per cent in August, up from 55.4 per cent in July. The worker-population ratio reached 52.8 per cent, its highest level since March. Rural employment continued to rise. Female labour-force participation reached 34.8 per cent, 1.1 percentage points above August 2025. Overall unemployment stood at 5.0 per cent.

A day earlier, another number had arrived.

Consumer inflation rose to 4.82 per cent in August. Rural inflation was 5.23 per cent. Rural food inflation was 6.13 per cent.

A week before that, CMIE wage data reported by the *Financial Express* showed average rural monthly wages falling from ₹21,250 in April to ₹19,922 in July. Combined with rising prices, the reported decline in real rural wages over those three months was 8.9 per cent. Urban real wages fell by 1.9 per cent.

Meanwhile, the World Bank estimates that extreme poverty in India fell from 27.1 per cent in 2011-12 to 2.6 per cent in 2023-24 using its $3.00-a-day 2021 PPP line. At the $4.20 lower-middle-income-country line, poverty fell from 57.7 to 16.1 per cent.

India’s economy grew 7.6 per cent in FY2025-26.

None of these figures needs to be false for the others to be true.

That is the point.

I think we have a problem in Indian development discussion with the category of “improvement”. We are often careful about the accuracy of an indicator and much less careful about the distance between the indicator and the thing we actually want to know.

Employment is a particularly good place to see this.

### More work

The August PLFS result extends a movement that was already visible in July. Overall labour-force participation rose from 54.4 per cent in June to 55.4 per cent in July and 55.6 per cent in August. Rural LFPR reached 58.2 per cent in August. The rural worker-population ratio reached 55.8 per cent, 1.3 percentage points higher than a year earlier.

These are meaningful changes.

They also answer fairly narrow questions.

The monthly PLFS uses Current Weekly Status. Someone who worked for at least one hour on any day during the reference week can enter the employed population under the survey’s activity-status rules. The unemployment rate then tells us what share of the labour force was unemployed.

This makes unemployment a poor standalone measure of economic security in a labour market where many people cannot afford prolonged open unemployment.

If a worker loses reasonably paid work and takes irregular casual work at a lower wage, measured employment survives.

If a woman joins a family enterprise without regular independent earnings, participation rises.

If a household responds to an income shock by sending another member into poorly paid work, LFPR may improve.

Employment statistics are doing their job in each case. The interpretation fails when we quietly turn “employed” into “doing well”.

Women make the problem particularly visible.

Female LFPR in India has risen dramatically over the longer PLFS period. Under usual status, it increased from 23.3 per cent in 2017-18 to 41.7 per cent in 2023-24.

I regard that movement as important. I do not regard 41.7 per cent as a sufficient measure of women’s economic progress.

Employment status matters. So do hours, earnings, occupation, working conditions, continuity of work, unpaid family labour and control over earnings.

There is an old temptation in development measurement to become attached to an indicator precisely when it starts moving in the direction we hoped it would. Female LFPR deserves more scrutiny now, not less.

### More employment, less purchasing power?

This is where the wage numbers become uncomfortable.

CMIE’s reported rural wage series shows nominal average monthly wages declining 6.25 per cent between April and July. Rural prices rose 2.9 per cent over the same period. Put together, real rural wages fell 8.9 per cent. The rural wage was about 66 per cent of the average urban wage in April and 62 per cent in July.

I would take this seriously. I would not yet call it evidence of a general rural wage collapse.

Three months is short. Agricultural labour demand is seasonal. The timing of the monsoon and kharif sowing matters. Composition matters too.

There is also a measurement problem hidden inside the apparently straightforward term “rural wage”.

The Labour Bureau’s Wage Rates in Rural India database reports daily wage rates by state, sex and occupation. It distinguishes ploughing, sowing, harvesting, horticulture, general agricultural labour, construction and other occupations. In January 2026, for example, Andhra Pradesh reported ₹613.31 per day for male sowing, planting and weeding workers and ₹443.87 for women doing the same category of work.

A national monthly wage average cannot tell us whether a decline comes from lower wages within occupations, movement between occupations, fewer days of paid work, a changing mix of workers, or some combination.

Those mechanisms have different policy implications.

The distinction between wage rates and earnings matters as well. A daily wage can rise while monthly earnings fall because there are fewer days of work.

This sounds elementary. Yet public discussion repeatedly slides between wages, earnings, employment and income as though they were interchangeable.

They are not.

### Then prices enter the room

The August CPI makes the question more pressing.

Headline inflation was 4.82 per cent. Rural inflation was 5.23 per cent. Food inflation was 5.95 per cent nationally and 6.13 per cent in rural India.

India is also measuring those prices differently now.

MoSPI moved the CPI base from 2012 to 2024 in February. The new series expanded price collection to 1,465 rural markets and 1,395 urban markets across 434 towns, added online markets and increased the all-India weighted basket from 299 to 358 items.

This is good statistical housekeeping. Consumption changes. An inflation basket should change with it.

But every CPI remains a weighted average of a consumption basket.

A household spending a large share of its budget on food experiences a 6 per cent increase in food prices differently from a household whose spending is distributed across housing, transport, education, services and discretionary consumption.

The official CPI is not claiming otherwise.

Our interpretation often does.

I find this distinction particularly important because inflation is routinely discussed as though the national headline rate describes a shared experience. It describes a price index.

Those are different propositions.

## Poverty fell. What exactly have we established?

The poverty numbers require a different sort of care.

The World Bank’s latest long-run estimates use the 2011-12 Consumption Expenditure Survey and the 2023-24 Household Consumption Expenditure Survey. At its revised extreme-poverty line of $3.00 per person per day in 2021 PPP terms, it estimates that poverty fell from 27.1 per cent to 2.6 per cent. At the $4.20 line used for lower-middle-income countries, the decline is from 57.7 to 16.1 per cent.

Those are very large reductions.

My reading is that the broad conclusion is difficult to dispute: India experienced substantial consumption-poverty reduction between 2011-12 and 2023-24.

I am considerably less persuaded by attempts to make the 2.6 per cent number carry the entire argument about deprivation in contemporary India.

The first reason is obvious. A poverty line creates a binary classification around a continuous distribution.

A household immediately above $3.00 has crossed the extreme-poverty threshold. Its economic security has not undergone a corresponding discontinuous transformation.

The World Bank’s own $4.20 estimate makes this visible. About 16.1 per cent of Indians remain below that threshold. That is roughly 232 million people in its calculation.

The second issue is survey comparability.

The 2023-24 HCES is a much better basis for contemporary consumption analysis than extrapolating indefinitely from 2011-12. But survey design changed over those twelve years. The World Bank therefore uses a harmonisation methodology to construct its long-term trend and has published a reproducibility package for the estimates.

This is exactly what serious poverty estimation should do. It also means the resulting time series is constructed, not simply read off two identical surveys.

That distinction is sometimes lost once the estimate enters political or public discussion.

The third issue is inequality.

MoSPI’s HCES reports that the Gini coefficient for monthly per-capita consumption expenditure fell from 0.283 in rural India in 2011-12 to 0.237 in 2023-24, and from 0.363 to 0.284 in urban India.

Again, this is evidence worth taking seriously.

It is evidence about the distribution of measured consumption.

It does not settle questions about wealth inequality, income inequality, unequal access to public services, indebtedness, insecurity, caste disparities, gendered control over resources or exposure to shocks.

The temptation here works in both directions. People sceptical of India’s growth story sometimes dismiss favourable consumption data too readily. People defending the growth story sometimes ask consumption poverty to prove much more than it measures.

Neither move improves the analysis.

## The South Asian version of the problem

India is hardly alone in this.

Bangladesh provides a useful counterexample because some of its headline indicators have moved in the opposite direction. The World Bank estimates that Bangladesh’s national poverty rate increased from 18.7 per cent in 2022 to 21.4 per cent in 2025. Inflation remained 8.5 per cent in FY2025-26, and wages among low-income workers failed to keep pace with prices.

There the signals line up more neatly: high inflation, weak real wages and rising poverty.

Sri Lanka is more interesting.

Its economy returned to growth after the crisis, expanding 5 per cent in 2024. Inflation fell sharply. Yet the World Bank estimated poverty at 24.5 per cent, roughly twice its 2019 level, while real wages remained below their 2019 level. Food prices had more than doubled between 2021 and 2024.

Macroeconomic recovery and household recovery were occurring on different clocks.

Nepal gives us another version. Extreme poverty fell from 21.5 per cent in 2011 to 3 per cent in 2023, while migration and remittances remain central to household livelihoods. The World Bank reports that 77.3 per cent of Nepali migrant workers are based in the Middle East.

What does a successful domestic development indicator mean when part of the mechanism producing it is the export of labour?

I do not mean that rhetorically. Remittances are income. Migration can improve welfare substantially. Workers make rational choices within the opportunities available to them.

But a fall in poverty generated partly through overseas earnings tells us something different about the domestic economy from a fall generated through rising local labour productivity and wages.

The poverty statistic alone cannot distinguish the development paths.

Across South Asia, the World Bank estimates that only 59 per cent of the working-age population is employed and female labour-force participation is about 32 per cent. Formal employment accounts for only around 10 per cent of the workforce.

This is why I am wary of treating South Asia’s high growth as though the jobs question will eventually resolve itself.

The region has been growing quickly for years. Its employment structure is still telling us something uncomfortable.

## Heat is an economic indicator too

There is another number I have been thinking about since it appeared in July.

The World Bank estimates that extreme heat is already costing South Asia labour equivalent to almost 31 million full-time jobs each year. By 2050, it estimates that heat could reduce the region’s economy by nearly 7 per cent.

The 2050 figure is a modelled projection and should be read as one. The 31-million-job-equivalent figure is also an estimate of labour-productivity losses, not a count of 31 million identifiable people who have lost jobs.

Still, the underlying problem is real and badly fitted to our conventional labour indicators.

Imagine a construction worker who remains employed through an extreme-heat period but works fewer effective hours, takes more breaks, loses days to illness, or earns less because output falls.

Employment status may not change.

Income can.

Health can.

Household welfare can.

The same problem appears in agriculture, street vending, delivery work, sanitation, transport and other occupations where workers cannot readily move labour indoors or shift working hours.

Climate exposure therefore creates another wedge between having work and what that work yields.

This matters for how we evaluate adaptation policy too. Counting heat action plans, cooling centres or alerts is straightforward. Estimating avoided illness, preserved earnings and distributional effects is much harder.

I increasingly think climate policy has the same measurement problem as employment policy: we count what administrative systems can see.

## Measurement matters: the indicator is not the construct

This issue began with a collection of numbers. It has ended up being about construct validity.

Suppose the construct we care about is something like **material economic security**.

We cannot observe it directly.

So we use proxies:

employment status
earnings
consumption
poverty status
inflation-adjusted income
assets
food expenditure
programme participation
debt
subjective economic security

Each captures something.

None is the construct.

Researchers know this in principle. In practice, the distinction often weakens somewhere between a survey table, a press release and a policy claim.

There are four questions I would ask whenever a development indicator appears to improve.

**What exactly was measured?**

PLFS unemployment is a labour-force measure. HCES MPCE is a consumption measure. CPI measures price change for a weighted basket. GDP measures production. Each has a definition, reference period and population.

**What mechanism could have produced the change?**

A rising female LFPR can reflect expanding opportunity, economic distress, agricultural seasonality or changing unpaid work. A lower poverty rate can result from higher earnings, transfers, remittances, lower dependency ratios or changes elsewhere in household consumption.

**Who moved?**

An all-India mean can improve while particular states, occupations or population groups deteriorate. Distribution is not an optional appendix to the average.

**What would I need to observe before making the stronger claim?**

This is the question I think we ask least often.

If employment rises, I want earnings and employment status.

If women’s participation rises, I want occupation, hours, earnings, unpaid work and control over income.

If poverty falls, I want the distribution around the threshold and measures of vulnerability to shocks.

If inflation falls, I want expenditure-specific incidence.

If a programme reaches more people, I want adequacy and outcomes.

The stronger claim may eventually be justified. It needs stronger evidence.

## Research praxis: build the claim before choosing the indicator

One habit I recommend to researchers I work with is embarrassingly simple.

Write the substantive claim before opening the dataset.

Suppose the claim is:

> Women’s economic position in rural India improved between two survey rounds.

Now list what would have to be true for you to believe it.

Would a rise in LFPR be enough?

Probably not.

Would increased paid employment be enough?

Better.

Would rising real earnings strengthen the claim?

Yes.

Would fewer women reporting unpaid family work matter?

Yes.

Would greater control over earnings matter?

Very much, although PLFS cannot answer it.

Would reduced time poverty alter the interpretation?

Again, yes.

You have now done something useful before running a regression. You have separated the construct from the indicators available to measure it.

This also exposes the missing-data problem early.

Many evaluation frameworks do the reverse. They begin with indicators because indicators are available. The programme’s substantive claim is then quietly rewritten to fit them.

A monitoring system records training completion, so completion becomes “capacity”.

An administrative database records registrations, so registration becomes “access”.

A livelihood programme records enterprises started, so enterprise creation becomes “economic empowerment”.

The mistake happens before estimation.

No econometric technique repairs a badly specified construct.

## What I think is worth reading

### 1. India Development Update, April 2026

The World Bank’s latest India update is worth reading partly because of a technical issue that received less attention than the growth forecast: India’s February 2026 GDP rebasing.

The base year moved to 2022-23, and the revised methodology incorporates more regular data on the household and informal sectors. MoSPI says annual sources such as ASUSE and PLFS will now contribute directly to household-sector estimates rather than relying as heavily on older benchmark surveys and proxy growth indicators.

This matters. The informal economy has always been one of the hardest parts of Indian GDP to estimate.

A rebasing exercise that changes our estimate of the size of that economy is not statistical housekeeping at the margins. It changes the denominator against which several other economic quantities are understood.

[India Development Update, April 2026](https://thedocs.worldbank.org/en/doc/4262e1e15b463ecb360cec4ad78cf062-0310012026/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 2. The World Bank’s India poverty reproducibility package

I wish reproducibility packages received a fraction of the attention that poverty headlines do.

The package provides the material behind the Bank’s updated 2011-12 to 2023-24 poverty series and specifies the $3.00 and $4.20 2021-PPP poverty lines used in the estimates.

If you disagree with a poverty estimate, this is where the interesting argument should begin: welfare aggregate, survey comparability, price adjustments, poverty line, treatment of transfers, sampling and sensitivity.

[India long-term poverty trend reproducibility package](https://reproducibility.worldbank.org/catalog/541?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 3. South Asia Economic Update: Working with Industrial Policy

I am less interested in its headline regional growth forecast than in the work on jobs and spatial wage differences.

The report finds persistent subnational wage premiums in South Asia’s larger economies even after controlling for worker characteristics. Better transport connections, skilled workforces, larger firms and service-sector activity are associated with higher residual wage premiums.

That is a useful corrective to national labour-market averages. Where a worker lives remains economically consequential after observable individual characteristics are taken into account.

[South Asia Economic Update, April 2026](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099919404082611180?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### 4. A Livable Future: Protecting Jobs and Growth from Extreme Heat in South Asia’s Cities

Read this alongside labour-market statistics rather than filing it under climate.

Its central contribution is to treat heat exposure as a labour and productivity problem. I would read the large aggregate estimates cautiously because they depend on modelling assumptions. The occupational logic is much harder to dismiss. Workers exposed to outdoor and poorly cooled environments experience heat through hours, output, illness and earnings.

That belongs in any serious discussion of South Asia’s future of work.

[World Bank material on extreme heat, jobs and growth in South Asia](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/07/28/extreme-heat-puts-south-asia-s-jobs-and-growth-at-risk-world-bank?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## What I’m thinking about

I keep coming back to the difference between **crossing a threshold and becoming secure**.

Development research uses thresholds everywhere.

Poverty lines. Minimum dietary diversity. Anaemia cut-offs. Learning proficiency. Minimum wages. Heat thresholds. Eligibility scores. Anthropometric cut-offs.

We need them. Policy cannot operate entirely in continuous distributions.

But thresholds have an odd effect on thought. Once someone crosses one, the classification changes abruptly while the person’s life may have changed only slightly.

A household at ₹99 against a ₹100 threshold and a household at ₹101 can be almost indistinguishable economically. In the dataset, one is poor and one is not.

The same problem appears when a child’s test score crosses a proficiency cut-off or a household becomes administratively eligible for a scheme.

This is hardly a new methodological discovery. What interests me is how readily the categorical result takes over the substantive discussion.

Perhaps we need to report **distance from thresholds** much more routinely.

For poverty, that means poverty gaps and vulnerability alongside headcounts.

For learning, score distributions alongside proficiency rates.

For nutrition, distributions around clinical cut-offs.

For programme eligibility, the density immediately above and below the administrative boundary.

A threshold is often necessary for a decision.

It is rarely enough for an explanation.

## Off the desk

One dataset rather than a book this time: the Labour Bureau’s **Wage Rates in Rural India** database.

It is not glamorous. The interface could be kinder. It is also the sort of public dataset that rewards wandering around in it.

Pick an occupation. Compare men and women. Move across states. Then change occupations.

The national category “rural labour” becomes difficult to sustain for very long.

In January 2026, Andhra Pradesh’s reported daily wage for male ploughing and tilling workers was ₹691.65. Male workers in sowing, planting, transplanting and weeding averaged ₹613.31; women in that category averaged ₹443.87.

Those differences are the labour market too.

## Where this leaves me

I started this issue with employment because the latest numbers looked good.

I still think they look good.

August brought higher labour-force participation, a higher worker-population ratio and continued gains in women’s participation. India has also experienced a very large reduction in consumption poverty over the longer term. Those findings should not be waved away because they complicate a preferred account of the economy.

I also think the evidence is telling us to ask a harder question.

**For a household, economic progress is experienced through what work pays, how predictable it is, what necessities cost, whether illness or heat interrupts earnings, whether debt is accumulating, whether public services work, and how far the household sits from the next shock.**

Our major statistical systems observe pieces of this remarkably well.

The mistake is ours when we make one piece stand for the whole.

That matters especially now because some of India’s indicators are moving in different directions. Employment is strengthening while recent rural wage data are weak. Food inflation has risen. Female participation is increasing without giving us a simple answer about the quality of women’s work. Consumption poverty has fallen substantially while a much larger population remains below a poverty line appropriate to lower-middle-income countries.

I do not read that as a contradiction in the data.

I read it as a warning about the question.

“Is India doing better?” is too imprecise to be useful.

Better on what measure, for whom, compared with when, and how far from insecurity?

Those are questions the data can begin to answer.

They require more than one number.

**For readers working with household or labour data in India or elsewhere in South Asia: what measure have you found changes the story most once you put it beside the headline indicator? I would particularly like to see state-level evidence, panel data or work that follows households around a poverty, employment or earnings threshold. If you send me something I use in a future Rundown, I will credit you.**
