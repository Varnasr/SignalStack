## 1. financial inclusion after the bank account

Ganesan, Priyadarshini, and Aishwarya Iyer. 2026. “The Winding Road from Financial Access to Financial Outcomes.” *Ideas for India*, 16 September 2026.

This is the piece I would read first.

Ganesan and Iyer take aim at a familiar measurement shortcut: treating financial access or even product use as evidence of financial inclusion having worked. Drawing on Dvara Research’s Financial Health Survey, they propose an Input-Output-Outcome-Context framework that separately measures product ownership, use, financial health, and household context.

The empirical application covers 4,000 households across 18 districts in seven Indian states. Households with both physical and digital financial infrastructure record higher financial-health scores than households with digital-only or physical-only access. Households with predictable incomes also perform better across the framework, while the relationship between informal finance and financial outcomes varies substantially by region.

The important point is methodological. An account, insurance policy or digital-payment channel is an input. Usage is an intermediate result. Neither establishes whether a household is better able to manage routine expenses, cope with shocks or plan ahead.

The current evidence is cross-sectional, so the observed associations do not establish causal effects. The framework itself is the more interesting contribution. It forces researchers and programmes to specify where they think change occurs along the chain.

This belongs in a future Methods Monday on proxy outcomes and theories of change.

## 2. the urban pollution source we still measure poorly

Ali, Sanjar, Bhavay Sharma, Sree Kumar Kumaraswamy, Vandana Tyagi, and Sreekanth Vakacherla. 2026. *City-Tier-Wise Open Waste Burning (OWB) Characteristics*. WRI India Working Paper, 17 September 2026.

Open waste burning sits awkwardly in urban air-pollution policy because it is visible enough to be familiar and poorly measured enough to be missing from many serious inventories.

WRI India surveys open waste burning across 11 Indian cities, covering winter and summer conditions and measuring incident density, estimated burned mass, waste composition, and associated PM2.5 and PM10 emissions. The authors explicitly identify the lack of comparable ground-level data as one reason the source remains poorly characterised.

This matters for city clean-air plans. An emissions inventory built from weak assumptions about informal burning can produce equally weak decisions about where scarce municipal money should go.

The paper provides a multicity descriptive baseline. It does not estimate the health burden attributable to open burning or identify the effect of particular waste-management interventions. The 11-city coverage also means the findings should not be casually converted into a national estimate.

Still, this fills a real evidence gap.

## 3. does cleaning roads actually reduce road dust?

Kumar, Ritesh, Sree Kumar Kumaraswamy, Sreekanth Vakacherla, and Vandana Tyagi. 2026. *Silt Load Measurement and Investigation of the Effectiveness of Road Dust Cleaning Mechanisms in Three Indian Cities*. WRI India Working Paper, 17 September 2026.

The companion WRI paper asks a refreshingly concrete question about an intervention Indian cities already spend money on.

The researchers measured road silt across different road types in Mumbai, Indore and Ahmedabad, compared manual, mechanical, water-based and other cleaning practices, and examined how quickly silt returned after cleaning in Mumbai and Ahmedabad. WRI describes the study explicitly as exploratory.

That qualification matters. Three cities cannot settle the question of which cleaning technology works across India. Road design, traffic, construction activity, season and local dust sources all affect re-accumulation.

The policy relevance is immediate, however. National Clean Air Programme funds are already being used for road-dust control, while evidence on the effectiveness of common cleaning practices remains thin.

This is the sort of mundane implementation research that deserves more attention. Programmes frequently evaluate whether equipment was purchased. Fewer ask what happened to the pollutant afterwards.

## 4. nearly a decade of federalism in Nepal, measured

Provincial and Local Governance Strengthening Programme. 2025. *Nepal: The State of Federalism Report 2025*. Released online 12 September 2026, with technical support from UNDP.

Nepal now has its first system-wide baseline of federal governance since the 2015 Constitution. It is intended to be repeated in 2027 and 2029, which makes the measurement architecture at least as interesting as the current scores.

The report combines quantitative and qualitative evidence across federal, provincial and local government, administrative indicators, interviews and citizen-perception data. The associated Survey of the Nepali People used multistage random sampling and Kish-grid respondent selection, with 12,500 respondents.

Two numbers capture the tension in the findings. Women hold around 41% of local elected positions, while more than 90% of subnational budgets remain dependent on federal grants and own-source revenue accounts for only 6–8%.

The report therefore gives us a useful distinction between political decentralisation, administrative capacity and fiscal autonomy. They need not move together.

As a baseline study, it can tell us where the system stands and how citizens perceive it. Attribution requires later rounds and a much harder identification problem. Changes between 2025 and 2029 cannot automatically be credited to federalism itself.

[Read the open-access report](https://www.undp.org/nepal/publications/state-federalism-report-2025?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

## 5. child labour and the targeting problem in Bangladesh’s safety nets

Moazzem, Khondaker Golam, Nishat Tasnim Nishu, and ASM Shamim Alam Shibly. 2025. *Strengthening Social Safety Nets for Child Labour Elimination in Bangladesh: Coverage, Gaps, and Policy Directions*. Centre for Policy Dialogue. Released online 15 September 2026.

A date caveat first. CPD posted this report this week, although its stated publication period is December 2025. I am including it as a newly available publication, rather than new fieldwork.

The central finding is simple and damaging: Bangladesh’s social-safety-net programmes were not designed around the factors along which child labour actually varies.

The study brings together national survey data, key-informant interviews and regional comparisons to identify gaps in programme coverage and targeting, then proposes a framework more explicitly tied to child-labour risk.

Its value lies in the mismatch it documents between programme architecture and policy objective. A transfer programme can reach poor households and still be badly designed as a child-labour intervention if eligibility rules, benefit size, timing and accompanying services ignore the mechanisms driving children into work.

The evidence supports a targeting and programme-design diagnosis. It does not establish the causal effect of existing safety-net participation on child labour.

## 6. why Bangladesh’s garment factories remain carbon-intensive

Mohammed, Sami, Khondaker Golam Moazzem, Maleehah Sabah Ali, and Mehadi Hasan Shamim. 2026. *The Carbon Stitch: Assessing Carbon Lock-In in the RMG Sector of Bangladesh*. Centre for Policy Dialogue. Released online 14 September 2026.

This is the second CPD item with a date qualification. The web release is new; CPD records the publication period as May 2026.

Using plant-level survey data and mixed methods, the authors examine why Bangladesh’s ready-made garment factories remain dependent on carbon-intensive production despite growing commercial and regulatory pressure to decarbonise. They find much greater uptake of relatively cheap energy-efficiency measures than of deeper capital-intensive changes. Financing costs, regulatory constraints, supply-chain arrangements and firm decision-making all appear in the explanation. Renewable-energy adoption also varies by factory size.

The interesting development question concerns firm heterogeneity. A decarbonisation requirement imposed on an industry does not imply that factories face comparable adjustment costs. Smaller producers may encounter a very different investment problem from large export factories with easier access to finance.

The mixed-methods design is suited to identifying constraints and mechanisms. It cannot by itself tell us how much emissions would fall under the financing and regulatory measures the authors recommend.

This is worth keeping beside last week’s Bangladesh future-of-work paper. Automation and decarbonisation are often discussed separately even though the same firms may be making both sets of capital-investment decisions.

## 7. whose implementation science?

Rayamajhee, Binod, Gopiram Syangtan, Bhupendra Lama, Saruna Ghimire, Brian Oldenburg, and Uday Narayan Yadav. 2026. “Building Bridges: A Framework for Redefining and Decolonizing Implementation Science in Low- and Middle-Income Countries.” *PLOS Global Public Health* 6(9): e0006155. Published 15 September 2026. doi: 10.1371/journal.pgph.0006155.

This is a critical review rather than a South Asia-specific empirical study, but several of its authors work in and on Nepal, and the methodological argument belongs in this list.

The authors examine the extent to which implementation-science frameworks developed in high-income settings travel to LMIC health systems. They propose a framework centred on locally adapted methods, local research leadership, funding arrangements, review structures and recognition of Indigenous and community knowledge.

I am less persuaded by new frameworks simply because they are new. Implementation science already has a framework proliferation problem. The test here will be whether this framework changes study design, decision rights, measures, budgets or interpretation when researchers actually apply it.

For now, treat this as an agenda-setting methodological paper. It proposes a way of thinking about implementation research; it does not provide empirical evidence that the proposed framework produces better implementation or health outcomes.

That makes it another possible Methods Monday piece, perhaps paired with the harder question of when creating a new framework adds analytical value.

## what I would keep

The Ganesan-Iyer piece is my pick this week because the argument extends well beyond financial inclusion. Development research repeatedly substitutes an administratively convenient indicator for the outcome in the theory of change. Their framework gives us a useful way to inspect where that substitution occurs.

I would also keep the two WRI papers together. They move air-pollution research toward questions cities can act on: which sources are being missed, whether interventions reduce them, and how quickly gains disappear.

The Nepal report is the substantial reference document of the week. Its real value may emerge over the next four years if the planned repeat measurements are carried out consistently.

I found no new South Asia-specific nutrition or WEE study within 12–18 September that was strong enough, clearly open access and sufficiently recent to earn a place. I would rather leave those categories empty than pad the edition.
