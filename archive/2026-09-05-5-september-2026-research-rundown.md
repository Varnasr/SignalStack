## FIVE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

### 1. A district-level measure of patriarchy predicts women’s reported experience of intimate partner violence

A new study uses NFHS-5 data for 61,180 women aged 18 to 49 and an India-specific structural patriarchy index to examine recent intimate partner violence. Women living in districts in the highest category of structural patriarchy had 1.92 times the adjusted odds of reporting IPV compared with women in districts in the lowest category, after accounting for caste, religion, wealth, demographic characteristics and individual gender attitudes. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12939-026-03010-x))

The study remains observational. District-level patriarchy may capture omitted institutional or social characteristics, and contemporaneous NFHS measures cannot establish temporal ordering. Its contribution lies in measuring gender hierarchy above the individual or household level, where much Indian IPV research has concentrated.

Why it matters: programmes that measure gender norms only through individual attitudes may miss structural conditions associated with violence.

[https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-03010-x](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12939-026-03010-x)

### 2. India now has its first imaging satellite designed for geosynchronous orbit

ISRO successfully launched EOS-05 aboard GSLV-F17 on 4 September. The 2,367 kg Earth-observation satellite was placed into its intended transfer orbit and is designed to provide frequent imagery over the Indian region from geosynchronous orbit. Earth-observation applications include agriculture, water management, environmental monitoring and disaster response. ([Business Standard](https://www.business-standard.com/india-news/isro-eos-05-launch-gslv-f17-geosynchronous-imaging-satellite-126090400257_1.html))

The development significance depends on the eventual data products, their temporal and spatial resolution, validation, and access arrangements. A technically successful satellite becomes useful public infrastructure when researchers, disaster agencies and sectoral departments can translate its observations into decisions.

Why it matters: frequent observation can reduce a persistent weakness in disaster and agricultural monitoring, where revisit times can leave substantial gaps during rapidly changing events.

### 3. The RBI is now trying to sterilise the liquidity created by its own dollar-mobilisation programme

On 4 September, the Reserve Bank of India announced a 30-day variable-rate reverse repo auction of ₹7 trillion for 7 September, with an unusual early-redemption option. Banking-system liquidity had reached a record surplus of ₹10.3 trillion on 3 September, largely because banks raised foreign-currency deposits under the RBI’s special programme and swapped those dollars with the central bank. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/indias-rbi-taps-longer-duration-reverse-repo-absorb-liquidity-offers-early-exit-2026-09-04/))

The redemption option is an attempt to solve a practical problem: banks have shown limited appetite for locking excess funds into longer-tenor reverse repos. It also weakens the RBI’s certainty about how much liquidity remains sterilised for the full period.

Why it matters: the foreign-exchange intervention that increased India’s reserve buffer has created a second policy problem in domestic money markets.

### 4. Sri Lanka’s drought is now translating directly into lost farm income and food insecurity

Rainfall deficits in several farming districts have reached 85 to 100 per cent below long-term averages, while water in hundreds of small tanks has fallen to around 10 per cent of normal capacity. Reuters reporting from Ampara and Monaragala documents crop losses, livestock water shortages, movement into casual labour and households skipping meals as income disappears. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/environment/el-nino-hit-sri-lanka-wells-run-dry-farmer-incomes-shrink-drought-deepens-2026-09-03/))

These are local observations rather than a nationally representative welfare survey, so the reporting cannot tell us the magnitude of income or consumption losses across Sri Lanka. It does show the transmission mechanism clearly: meteorological drought becomes a livelihood shock through irrigation failure, crop loss and indebtedness.

Why it matters: drought monitoring that stops at rainfall and reservoir levels misses the household outcomes that determine whether climate stress becomes a poverty shock.

[https://www.reuters.com/business/environment/el-nino-hit-sri-lanka-wells-run-dry-farmer-incomes-shrink-drought-deepens-2026-09-03/](https://www.reuters.com/business/environment/el-nino-hit-sri-lanka-wells-run-dry-farmer-incomes-shrink-drought-deepens-2026-09-03/)

### 5. The Supreme Court has widened the accountability obligations surrounding teacher education

The Supreme Court has upheld the National Council for Teacher Education’s authority to require recognised teacher-education institutions to file annual Performance Appraisal Reports. The judgment reverses a 2023 Delhi High Court ruling and treats performance reporting as part of NCTE’s statutory responsibility for regulating teacher preparation. ([The Indian Express](https://indianexpress.com/article/legal-news/supreme-court-upholds-ncte-notice-seeking-appraisal-reports-from-teachers-education-institutes-10862404/))

The Court also placed teacher-education institutions and NCTE within the chain of institutions responsible for the quality of elementary education. Performance reports themselves will tell us little unless the data are verified, published in usable form and linked to credible regulatory action.

Why it matters: teacher quality debates often begin after recruitment. This judgment moves regulatory scrutiny upstream to the institutions that train teachers.

## ONE PAPER WORTH READING

### Groundwater can rearrange inequality, rather than simply deepen an existing hierarchy

Tanvi Agrawal asks how changing groundwater conditions alter access to agricultural resources and social advantage in the Kaveri Delta. The study is unusually well suited to that question. Fieldwork in two adjoining Tamil Nadu villages combined 103 interviews with residents, 21 interviews with officials, focus groups and participant observation with a census of agricultural tubewells, aquifer mapping, electrical-conductivity measurements from 69 wells and spatial data on land, caste and cropping. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10040-026-03148-6))

The study finds recent, spatially uneven groundwater salinisation following decades of intensive irrigated agriculture. Earlier gains from tubewells were concentrated among relatively advantaged landholders. Yet the geography of later salinisation does not map neatly onto the caste hierarchy: some middle-caste farmers whose groundwater has turned saline now face greater agricultural losses than Dalit cultivators in pockets where freshwater remains available. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10040-026-03148-6))

The main caution is scale. This is close-grained research in one village setting, designed for explanation rather than population inference. That is also its value. Combining physical measurements with social and institutional evidence shows why environmental inequality cannot always be inferred from household characteristics alone.

## ONE NUMBER

### ₹10.3 trillion

That was the banking-system liquidity surplus on 3 September, according to the data reported when the RBI announced its new absorption operation. It is a record, and the source of the money matters: much of the surplus followed foreign-currency deposits raised under the RBI’s special scheme and swapped into rupees at the central bank. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/world/india/indias-rbi-taps-longer-duration-reverse-repo-absorb-liquidity-offers-early-exit-2026-09-04/))

The figure therefore should not be interpreted as evidence of an equivalent improvement in ordinary domestic credit conditions. It is partly the rupee-side consequence of an exchange-rate and reserve-management intervention.

## POLICY WATCH

### The Supreme Court has given education regulators considerable room to demand institutional data

In *National Council for Teachers Education v. Association of NCTE Approved Colleges Trust*, decided on 3 September, the Supreme Court held that NCTE and its Executive Committee may require recognised teacher-education institutions to submit annual Performance Appraisal Reports. The Court treated collection of such information as falling within NCTE’s statutory and ancillary regulatory powers and set aside the Delhi High Court judgment that had invalidated the requirement. ([Indian Kanoon](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/93988461/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

There is a broader administrative-law point here. The judgment accepts that regulators need enough incidental authority to collect information required to perform their statutory functions. For education policy, the immediate question becomes empirical: what does NCTE collect, how comparable are those reports across institutions, and what happens when a report identifies poor performance? Reporting requirements can create accountability. They can also become another compliance form.

## EDITORIAL NOTE

### Climate vulnerability is partly a problem of measurement scale

The Kaveri groundwater paper and the Sri Lankan drought reporting point to the same analytical problem. Climate indicators are usually aggregated at scales that make distributional consequences difficult to see.

A district can have one rainfall anomaly while farms within it have radically different irrigation access. A village can experience groundwater decline while individual wells sit in different aquifers or salinity zones. Two households with similar landholdings can therefore experience the same meteorological shock quite differently. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10040-026-03148-6))

This matters for targeting. District drought declarations, average groundwater levels and seasonal rainfall departures remain useful administrative statistics. They are weak proxies for household exposure when water access is spatially uneven and mediated by land ownership, caste, infrastructure and the ability to switch livelihoods.

The next generation of climate vulnerability measurement in India needs finer physical data joined to social data. EOS-05 may eventually add another useful layer. The hard part remains linking what a satellite sees to who bears the loss.
