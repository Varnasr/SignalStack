## The village was never the real question

*Gandhi imagined economic and political power moving closer to ordinary people. Ambedkar asked what happens when the local community itself is unequal. Rural India still lives with the problem they identified from opposite directions.*

Gandhi Jayanti has not been particularly kind to Gandhi’s economic thought. What survives in public commemoration is usually a small repertoire of symbols: the charkha, khadi, sanitation, simplicity and, above all, the village. Detached from the arguments in which Gandhi placed them, these can make his economics seem either quaint or easily admirable. Neither reading is especially useful.

Gandhi’s interest in the village belonged to a much larger argument about the organisation of economic and political power. He distrusted an industrial order in which ownership, production and decision-making became increasingly concentrated. He worried about forms of technological change that could increase output while leaving large numbers of people without adequate work. His preference for local production was therefore connected to employment, control over productive assets and the distribution of economic power. In the writings collected as *Village Swaraj*, he argued that the means of producing basic necessities should remain under broad social control and that people should be able to obtain sufficient work to support themselves. [Mahatma Gandhi Website](https://www.mkgandhi.org/village_swaraj/preface.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Village Swaraj was not simply a plea to preserve rural India as Gandhi found it. His description of an ideal village included food production, clean water, schools, public spaces, cooperative economic activity, elected local government, and the abolition of caste hierarchy and untouchability. He imagined a village that was substantially capable of meeting its basic needs while remaining interdependent with the world beyond it. [Mani Bhavan](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/gandhian-philosophy/philosophy-village-idealvillage.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That distinction matters because the Indian village as it existed was precisely where Ambedkar’s objection began.

## Ambedkar’s problem with the village

In the Constituent Assembly on 4 November 1948, B. R. Ambedkar responded sharply to members who wanted the Constitution to give greater importance to traditional village communities. His description of the village as a “sink of localism” is now frequently quoted. The more important sentence for our purposes comes immediately afterwards. Ambedkar welcomed the Draft Constitution because it had discarded the village and “adopted the individual as its unit.” [Constitution of India](https://www.constitutionofindia.net/debates/04-nov-1948/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The disagreement was therefore deeper than a dispute over whether village life was desirable.

Gandhi began with the possibility of transforming the community. His ideal village would have neither caste hierarchy nor untouchability and would govern itself through democratic cooperation. Ambedkar began from the social relations of the village that actually existed. Caste was not an unfortunate feature that could simply be removed from the village model. It structured property, labour, residence, social status and political power. A political system that devolved authority to the village without changing those relations could give greater authority to the people who already dominated it.

This is one reason the Gandhi-Ambedkar disagreement remains useful for development policy. It identifies a problem that has never been solved simply by choosing centralisation or decentralisation.

Moving power closer to people can make institutions more responsive. It can also move power closer to existing systems of local domination.

The important question is not therefore whether decentralisation is good. It is how power is distributed within the unit to which authority is being decentralised.

## India eventually tried to do both

Independent India’s system of rural local government can be read, cautiously, as an attempt to live with both propositions.

The 73rd Constitutional Amendment gave Panchayati Raj institutions constitutional status and established elected local government across rural India. It also required reservation of seats for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes and reserved at least one-third of seats and chairperson positions for women. Many states subsequently increased women’s reservation to 50 per cent.

That second part is important. Decentralisation says that decisions should move downwards. Political reservation asks who will be present when those decisions are made.

There is now substantial evidence that this institutional design has had consequences.

One of the best-known studies remains Raghabendra Chattopadhyay and Esther Duflo’s analysis of randomly reserved village council leadership positions in West Bengal and Rajasthan. Using data from 265 village councils, they found that women leaders changed the composition of local public spending, investing more in public goods that were particularly relevant to women’s expressed priorities. Representation affected policy. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00539.x?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Later research has found effects extending beyond the period in which seats are reserved. Evidence from Jharkhand, for example, suggests that councils previously exposed to gender quotas continue to elect more women after the quota ceases to bind. Other research has found effects on women’s political participation beyond local government, although these effects vary by level of government and institutional setting. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305750X23001109?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

None of this means that a reserved seat automatically redistributes political power. Research on Panchayati Raj has also documented constraints created by caste, gender relations, institutional design and differences in representatives’ ability to exercise authority. [Korea Journal Central](https://journal.kci.go.kr/jrsd_ipaid/archive/articleView?artiId=ART001615735&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That combination of findings is more useful than either a celebratory or dismissive account of decentralisation. Institutions can change who participates and what governments do. The social relations surrounding those institutions continue to matter.

## MGNREGA contains the same tension

Consider MGNREGA.

It is difficult to imagine a less purely Gandhian programme. The employment guarantee is created by national legislation, financed overwhelmingly beyond the village and supported by administrative and digital systems operating at enormous scale.

Yet its implementation also gives local institutions a significant role. Gram panchayats are involved in planning and implementing works, and the programme’s social-audit architecture attempts to create a form of public accountability in which expenditure, works and worker entitlements can be examined locally. The government’s own social-audit system records works, expenditure, wage payments and entitlements at gram-panchayat level. [MGNREGA](https://mnregaweb4.nic.in/netnrega/SocialAuditFindings/SA-GPReport.aspx?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The design is interesting because it does not assume that proximity itself creates accountability. It attempts to build an institution through which local decisions can be inspected.

And even that is insufficient by itself. Research from Karnataka, for example, found that formal evidence that social audits had occurred did not necessarily mean workers had participated effectively. In the gram panchayats studied, the researchers found that local elites could influence the process and exclude poorer labourers. [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/246546990_How_Effective_are_Social_Audits_under_MGNREGS_Lessons_from_Karnataka?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

This is almost a textbook example of the problem Ambedkar identified.

A gram sabha is closer to a worker than a ministry in Delhi. That does not guarantee that the worker has equal power inside the gram sabha.

The distinction is important for development practice because we often use words such as “community”, “participation” and “local ownership” without specifying who inside the community participates, whose preferences determine the outcome, or who has enough social power to disagree publicly.

A community is not a unitary actor.

## The economic question is different, but connected

Gandhi’s economics raises a second problem that deserves more attention than the charkha usually receives.

He was concerned with employment as well as production. His argument for dispersed production rested partly on the proposition that an economic system in a labour-abundant country should provide people with enough work to live. His opposition to concentrated control over the production of basic necessities followed from the same concern. [Mahatma Gandhi Website](https://www.mkgandhi.org/village_swaraj/preface.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

Taken literally as an industrial strategy for India in 2026, much of this is difficult to sustain. A modern economy cannot be organised as hundreds of thousands of substantially self-sufficient village economies. Modern medicine, universities, railways, electricity grids, pharmaceutical production, telecommunications and advanced manufacturing depend on specialisation, large networks and forms of coordination extending far beyond a village.

Scale produces real gains.

Cities also matter for reasons that a romantic account of rural community can obscure. Migration can provide higher earnings, education and new social possibilities. Leaving a village may be particularly important for people whose opportunities are constrained by local caste or gender relations. Rural development should not be judged by whether it succeeds in preventing people from moving.

But rejecting village self-sufficiency does not settle Gandhi’s economic question.

India can produce more without creating enough secure and adequately paid employment. Agricultural productivity can increase while labour demand falls. Platform economies can create new work while transferring economic risk to workers. Automation can raise output while reducing the labour required to produce it.

The contemporary question is therefore not whether India should return to village production. It is whether growth in output is sufficient evidence of economic progress when the distribution of work, income, risk and economic power moves differently.

That is a question development economics recognises perfectly well, even if we no longer use Gandhi’s vocabulary to ask it.

## The village economy has already changed

There is another reason not to turn Gandhi’s village into a contemporary policy prescription: the rural economy itself no longer corresponds to the agricultural village that dominates much public imagination.

Rural households can combine farming, casual labour, salaried employment, public works, self-employment and remittances. Workers move between villages, small towns and large cities. Goods, credit, information and public benefits travel through networks extending far beyond local boundaries. Mobile phones and digital payments have altered the economic geography of rural life.

The rural and urban economies are not separate systems.

This matters because self-reliance can easily become a misleading development objective if it means reducing the opportunities available outside a locality. A village from which nobody needs to migrate is not necessarily more developed than one whose residents can move freely and obtain better work elsewhere.

The better question concerns choice. Can people remain without being trapped by the absence of alternatives? Can they leave without losing access to rights and public services? Can local economies provide viable livelihoods without making mobility a sign of failure?

That formulation is quite different from either preserving the village or abandoning it.

## Climate change gives decentralisation another meaning

There is, however, one part of Gandhi’s concern with local production and restraint that looks different in an era of climate change.

Development has spent decades asking how economies can produce and consume more. Climate change forces an additional question: what material and ecological systems make that production possible?

Food, water, energy and housing are unusually local when something goes wrong. A national electricity grid may be centrally organised, but a household experiences a blackout locally. Food markets may be national or global, but crop failure begins in particular fields. Climate models operate at large scales, while heat stress is experienced by particular workers in particular places.

Local capacity therefore matters for resilience.

This is not an argument for economic autarky. It is an argument for examining dependence more carefully.

A community dependent on a single crop, employer, water source or transport connection may be efficient under ordinary conditions and extremely vulnerable when conditions change. Diversification and redundancy can look inefficient until a shock occurs.

Here the Gandhian suspicion of an economy organised solely around increasing production intersects with a contemporary problem he could not have formulated in our terms. Economic systems have environmental costs, and those costs are unevenly distributed.

The question is not merely how much an economy produces. It is who receives the benefits, who performs the work, and who absorbs the ecological cost.

## The measurement problem

There is also a measurement problem here, which is where this argument connects most directly with the concerns of Research Rundown.

Suppose we wanted to know whether a village had become more capable of governing its own development.

Income per capita would tell us something. It would not tell us enough.

We would want to know about employment and wages, but also land ownership and indebtedness. We would need measures of drinking water, sanitation, healthcare and schooling, but also whether access differed by caste or settlement. Women’s labour-force participation would matter, but so would wages, unpaid work, control over earnings and freedom of movement.

Political representation could be counted. Effective political authority would be harder to measure.

A village could elect a woman sarpanch while decisions were exercised informally by male relatives. It could have a functioning gram panchayat while particular hamlets remained poorly served. Average household income could increase while land concentration worsened. Infrastructure could improve while groundwater deteriorated.

None of these contradictions is unusual. They are what development data look like once we stop asking one indicator to represent an entire social condition.

This is also why the individual matters.

Household income is not individual income. Household food security does not tell us how food is distributed within the household. Women’s employment does not tell us who controls their wages. Village-level access to water does not establish equal access across neighbourhoods or caste groups.

Ambedkar’s insistence on the individual as the constitutional unit has an interesting methodological echo here. Development researchers repeatedly need aggregate units. We sample households, villages, districts and states because research and administration require them. But improvements measured at those levels cannot simply be assumed to reach every person within them.

The average village does not experience development.

People do.

## Gandhi’s ideal village also presents an evaluation problem

There is a further difficulty with Gandhi’s village that becomes apparent when we read his description closely.

His ideal village already contains many of the outcomes that political and economic institutions are supposed to produce. It has democratic government, cooperation, clean water, education, and no caste hierarchy or untouchability. [Mani Bhavan](https://www.gandhi-manibhavan.org/gandhian-philosophy/philosophy-village-idealvillage.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That makes it morally attractive, but analytically difficult.

If the village is already free of the social inequalities that make local government problematic, decentralisation becomes easier to defend. The harder policy problem is how democratic local government functions while those inequalities still exist.

Ambedkar begins closer to that problem.

He asks us to start with unequal society rather than assume that equality has already been produced.

This is why the disagreement cannot be resolved by saying Gandhi imagined an egalitarian village and therefore had already answered Ambedkar’s criticism. The disagreement concerns the route to democratic community as much as its desired form.

Can local democracy dismantle entrenched hierarchy?

Or does equality require rights and institutions strong enough to protect individuals against local power before meaningful local democracy becomes possible?

India’s experience suggests that these are not necessarily alternatives. Constitutional rights, political reservation, nationally financed entitlements and decentralised institutions can operate together. Their relationship is often uncomfortable, and implementation varies considerably, but that may be precisely the point.

The answer to excessive centralisation need not be unprotected localism.

## What remains useful in Gandhi

I would not use Gandhi as a guide to the industrial structure India should adopt in 2026. Nor would I use the self-sufficient village as a benchmark for rural development.

But several of his economic concerns remain worth taking seriously.

One is concentration. Who owns productive assets and how much economic power follows from that ownership?

Another is employment. Does technological and economic change produce livelihoods as well as output?

A third is scale. Which decisions benefit from being made locally, and which require institutions operating at much larger levels?

A fourth is ecological restraint. How much production and consumption can an economy sustain, and who bears their environmental costs?

These questions survive even when Gandhi’s answers do not.

Ambedkar makes the inquiry harder by requiring us to examine power within the community as carefully as power above it. Decentralisation can bring government closer to citizens, but distance from Delhi is not a measure of democracy. A decision made in the village can still exclude the person standing outside its dominant caste, class or gender relations.

The evidence from Panchayati Raj is useful precisely because it refuses a simple conclusion. Political reservation has changed representation and, in some settings, public investment and subsequent political participation. At the same time, local institutions continue to operate inside unequal social structures. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2004.00539.x?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That is not evidence that decentralisation has succeeded or failed. It tells us that institutional design changes incentives and participation ,while social power does not disappear when an institution is redesigned.

## The village was never the real question

The interesting argument between Gandhi and Ambedkar is therefore not about whether villages are good places.

It is about power.

Gandhi asks what happens when economic and political power becomes too distant and too concentrated. Ambedkar asks what happens when power is moved closer to people without confronting hierarchy among those people.

Development policy still moves between these two problems.

Centralised systems can achieve scale, redistribution and enforceable rights that local institutions cannot. They can also become unresponsive to local conditions and difficult for citizens to influence.

Local institutions can use knowledge that central governments do not possess and provide citizens with more direct routes into decision-making. They can also reproduce the inequalities of the societies in which they operate.

Neither scale solves the problem of power by itself.

That seems a more useful way to return to Gandhi on 2 October than asking whether India has remained faithful to his vision of the village. India has changed too much for that to be an especially productive test.

The harder question is whether development has answered the problems that made Gandhi suspicious of concentrated economic power in the first place: insecure work, unequal ownership, dependence and the distance between ordinary people and the institutions that shape their lives.

Ambedkar requires us to add another test. When power is redistributed, who actually receives it?

That is where the argument becomes contemporary.

And it is why the Indian village remains interesting, not as a model to which India should return, but as a place in which some of the central problems of development are unusually easy to see.
