For much of development economics, the route from poverty to prosperity had a familiar shape. Workers moved out of low-productivity agriculture, manufacturing expanded, productivity rose, and economies became richer. East Asia supplied the most persuasive examples.

Gordon Hanson, Dani Rodrik and Rohan Sandhu think that route is becoming much harder to follow. Their new essay, “Rethinking Development,” in the September 2026 issue of *Finance & Development*, asks what development policy should look like when manufacturing can no longer absorb workers at anything like its historical scale. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/rethinking-development-gordon-hanson?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### The argument

The starting point is employment.

Hanson, Rodrik and Sandhu project that roughly two-thirds of workers in developing economies outside China will, a decade from now, be in jobs that require neither a college education nor exposure to international trade. Think retail, food services, domestic work, personal services, small businesses and other largely local activities. Many are informal and have low productivity. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/rethinking-development-gordon-hanson?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

That matters because two standard prescriptions in development policy, education and export-oriented industrialisation, reach only part of this labour force.

Manufacturing remains economically important. The authors point to productivity gains, learning effects, investment and national security. Their narrower claim is about jobs. Manufacturing has become increasingly capital-intensive and cannot be expected to absorb the growing labour forces of developing countries on the scale that it once did. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/rethinking-development-gordon-hanson?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The development question therefore changes. How do you raise productivity and earnings in the sectors where most people actually work?

### Taking services seriously

This is where the essay gets interesting.

Low-productivity services have often been treated as the residue of incomplete structural transformation: workers sell food, drive taxis, run tiny shops or provide personal services because the modern economy has failed to absorb them.

Hanson, Rodrik and Sandhu ask us to treat these activities as a central object of development policy instead.

Their prescription has two sides. Workers need better pathways from secondary education into occupations, including vocational and technical training. Small firms need policies directed specifically at productivity: management support, technology, marketing assistance, links with larger firms and other public inputs suited to their actual constraints. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/rethinking-development-gordon-hanson?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

This extends an argument Rodrik and Sandhu have been developing for some time. Their earlier work on labour-absorbing services considers policies ranging from public inputs for smaller firms to technologies that complement lower-skilled labour and vocational programmes combined with employment support. ([National Bureau of Economic Research](https://www.nber.org/papers/w32738?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### Where I would push the argument

I am persuaded by the diagnosis more than I am by the implied policy transition.

There is a real danger in moving too quickly from “most workers will be in services” to “therefore services must become the new engine of development.” Employment shares tell us where the problem is. They do not tell us whether these sectors possess the productivity dynamics that made manufacturing historically unusual.

A street vendor becoming 15 per cent more productive is good for the vendor. The harder question is whether millions of such improvements can generate sustained economy-wide productivity growth, rising wages, technological learning and movement into progressively more productive activities.

The authors recognise this problem. They point to emerging evidence of productivity growth within some nontraded services and make experimentation central to their policy proposal. But the empirical burden remains substantial. We know considerably more about how manufacturing generated structural change than we do about whether fragmented, often informal service sectors can reproduce enough of those dynamics. ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2026/09/rethinking-development-gordon-hanson?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### The India question

For India, this argument is difficult to ignore.

India has long presented an awkward case for the conventional structural-transformation story. Its economy moved unusually quickly toward services without first passing through an East Asian-scale manufacturing employment transition. At the same time, a large share of employment remains informal and productivity differences across workers and firms are enormous.

That makes the authors’ question particularly relevant. If manufacturing is unlikely to absorb enough Indian workers, improving productivity where people already work becomes unavoidable.

I would still resist treating this as an alternative to industrial policy. India needs both. Manufacturing can matter enormously for productivity, exports and technological capabilities even if its employment share never approaches earlier East Asian levels. The harder policy problem is how productivity gains in manufacturing and high-productivity services connect to the much larger labour force working elsewhere.

That distributional connection is where the essay could have gone further.

### Why read it?

Because it poses a development question that is becoming increasingly difficult to avoid.

For decades, economists could point to historical experience when asked how poor countries become rich. The sequence differed across countries, but industrialisation occupied a privileged place in most successful accounts.

Hanson, Rodrik and Sandhu are asking what happens when the historical ladder is still there, but can carry far fewer workers.

Their answer is provisional. That is precisely what makes the essay useful. The problem is clearer than the solution.
