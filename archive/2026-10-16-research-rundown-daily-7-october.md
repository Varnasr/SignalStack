India’s statistical system is beginning to account for resources and activities that conventional economic indicators have historically captured inadequately. On 7 October, the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation released a new strategy for environmental-economic accounting covering 2026–2030. A national household travel survey published two days earlier provides detailed information about commuting patterns, including substantial differences between men and women. Developments in banking oversight, healthcare regulation and rural water supply raise further questions about the quality of administrative information and its usefulness for public policy.

## FIVE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

### 1. India has released a new environmental-economic accounting strategy for 2026–2030.

The Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI) released its *Strategy for Environmental Economic Accounts in India 2026–2030* on 7 October. The document builds on work initiated under the ministry’s previous strategy for 2022–2026 and identifies priorities for expanding India’s environmental accounts.

The proposed areas include forests, minerals, soil resources, land, water, carbon stocks, biodiversity and environmentally related economic activities. The ministry has already undertaken work on wetlands, ocean accounts, croplands, energy, water quality and pollination services.

Environmental-economic accounting attempts to establish consistent statistical relationships between economic production and changes in natural resources. The United Nations System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA) provides the international framework for this work.

Consider groundwater extraction. Agricultural production that depends on groundwater contributes to national income, while the depletion of the underlying resource presents a separate economic and environmental problem. Conventional measures of production provide limited information about the sustainability of the resource base supporting that activity.

Environmental accounts can help describe these relationships through physical measures of resource stocks and flows, supplemented by monetary estimates where appropriate. Their construction requires information from several sources, including administrative records, field surveys, remote sensing and scientific assessments.

The methodological difficulties are considerable. Environmental data differ in geographical coverage, reporting periods, classification systems and measurement procedures. Measuring changes in the condition of an ecosystem also requires decisions about which characteristics should be included and how those characteristics should be interpreted.

The new strategy identifies data gaps and proposes further work to develop environmental accounts at national and state levels. Its practical value will depend on the publication of comparable statistical series, transparent methods and sufficiently detailed information for policy analysis.

For India, where groundwater depletion, land degradation and ecosystem loss have direct consequences for livelihoods and economic production, these accounts could become an important part of environmental policy assessment.

### 2. India’s first National Household Travel Survey reveals substantial gender differences in work-related travel.

The National Statistical Office released findings from India’s first National Household Travel Survey on 5 October. Fieldwork was conducted between July 2025 and June 2026 and covered 492,023 households and 2,034,371 people across 14,274 villages and 10,377 urban blocks.

The survey collected information about journeys undertaken for employment, education and other purposes, including travel distance, duration, expenditure and modes of transport.

Among workers aged fifteen and above, 57.6 per cent reported work-related journeys exceeding one kilometre. The corresponding proportion was 61.9 per cent among male workers and 41.9 per cent among female workers, a difference of twenty percentage points.

The survey also found that two-wheelers were the primary mode of transport for 42.6 per cent of workers who reported qualifying work-related journeys. Walking accounted for 28.3 per cent.

These figures have different denominators. The gender-specific percentages describe the proportion of workers undertaking journeys exceeding one kilometre. The transport-mode percentages describe the distribution of primary transport modes among workers who made those journeys.

This distinction matters for interpretation.

The lower proportion of women reporting work-related travel may reflect differences in occupations, workplace locations, employment arrangements and household responsibilities. Restrictions on mobility and difficulties accessing transport may also contribute.

The survey establishes the observed differences. Identifying their causes requires further analysis.

The definition of travel introduces another consideration. Journeys shorter than one kilometre were excluded. This is relevant for employment undertaken near home and for short trips associated with domestic work and caregiving.

Commuting expenditure provides additional evidence. Workers travelling to fixed workplaces spent an average of ₹785 per month. The corresponding expenditure was ₹1,044 in urban areas and ₹612 in rural areas.

These amounts should be examined relative to earnings and household income. The financial burden of commuting can be substantial for low-paid workers even when their expenditure is below the national average.

The survey provides an opportunity to study whether transport availability and affordability influence access to employment. Its greatest contribution may emerge when the underlying microdata are analysed alongside information about employment, income, household responsibilities and geographical location.

### 3. Banks will have to report Scheduled Caste beneficiaries separately under major financial schemes.

On 7 October, the National Commission for Scheduled Castes announced that the Department of Financial Services had instructed State Level Bankers’ Committees and their Union Territory equivalents to report assistance provided to Scheduled Caste beneficiaries under major government financial schemes.

The directions followed an intervention by the Commission on 24 September. It had observed that existing committee reports did not adequately record the proportion of Scheduled Caste families and beneficiaries receiving assistance.

The schemes identified include Pradhan Mantri Mudra Yojana, Stand-Up India, the Prime Minister’s Employment Generation Programme, PM SVANidhi and other forms of MSME credit assistance.

Under directions issued on 5 October, committees must include scheme-wise and bank-wise information on the number and percentage of Scheduled Caste beneficiaries in their periodic reviews. The Department of Financial Services has also requested an action-taken report by 9 October.

This is a useful administrative change. Financial inclusion is frequently assessed through aggregate indicators such as the number of accounts opened, loans sanctioned and amounts disbursed. These measures provide information about programme activity, while their distribution across social groups is necessary for examining inequalities in access.

The proposed reporting system could establish where assistance is concentrated and identify banks or schemes with relatively low Scheduled Caste participation.

Its analytical value will depend on the information collected.

The proportion of Scheduled Caste beneficiaries among approved borrowers, for example, should be interpreted alongside the composition of eligible applicants, application rates and loan approval rates. Loan amounts matter as well. Similar beneficiary shares can coexist with substantial differences in the average value of credit received.

There is also a need to distinguish applicants, sanctioned borrowers and people who actually receive funds.

The Commission’s intervention could improve monitoring of financial inclusion if these categories are clearly defined and consistently reported. Publication of comparable data would allow independent researchers to examine differences between banks, schemes and geographical areas.

### 4. India has introduced new registration and training requirements for allied healthcare professionals.

The National Commission for Allied and Healthcare Professions announced regulatory measures on 7 October covering institutional assessment, professional registration and training standards.

The measures include autonomous boards under State Allied and Healthcare Councils, a Central Assessment Committee for areas without functioning State Councils, and a national registration framework for allied healthcare professionals.

The Registration of Allied and Healthcare Professionals Regulations, published in the Gazette on 15 September, establish State and Central Registers with unique identification numbers and publicly verifiable digital certificates.

Registration will remain valid for five years, subject to renewal and continuing professional development requirements.

The Commission has also introduced a competency-based curriculum for the Diploma in Medical Radiology and Imaging Technology. The two-year programme includes six months of clinical internship and will become mandatory for relevant institutions from the 2027–28 academic year.

These developments concern a substantial section of India’s healthcare workforce. Allied healthcare professionals undertake diagnostic, rehabilitation and clinical support activities that affect the availability and quality of services in hospitals, primary healthcare facilities and diagnostic centres.

A common registration framework could improve information about professional qualifications and workforce availability. Public registers may also help patients and employers verify credentials.

The immediate implementation problem concerns professionals already working in the sector whose qualifications differ from the prescribed standards. The Commission has indicated that it is developing arrangements for provisional registration and bridge courses for eligible practitioners.

The design of these arrangements will influence how existing workers enter the new regulatory system.

For health workforce planning, registration data will be useful if they can be connected with geographical location, professional specialisation and employment status. The number of registered professionals provides only a partial account of the services actually available within a district.

### 5. Jal Jeevan Mission 2.0 is turning its attention to the reliability of rural water services.

The Department of Drinking Water and Sanitation held a regional review meeting on Jal Jeevan Mission 2.0 at IIM Bangalore on 7 October.

The discussions concerned water-source sustainability, service reliability, water quality monitoring, operational expenditure and the management of rural drinking water infrastructure.

According to the department, more than 15.96 crore rural households now have tap water connections, compared with 3.27 crore households when the mission began in August 2019.

The expansion represents a substantial increase in reported household access. Its consequences for water security depend on the performance of the systems supplying those connections.

Officials reported that pipeline leakages and breakages were leading causes of service interruptions identified through *Jal Seva Adhayan* assessments. Power failures and low-voltage conditions also affected water delivery.

The department has consequently called for greater attention to maintenance, financial sustainability and water quality surveillance.

These operational problems have direct implications for households. Interruptions in piped supply may require families to store water, purchase water or obtain it from alternative sources. The resulting costs can vary considerably across locations and income groups.

For programme monitoring, the availability of a tap connection needs to be accompanied by information about service performance.

Useful indicators include the number and duration of supply interruptions, water quality test results, repair times and the expenditure households incur when piped services fail.

Household reports can provide an independent source of evidence on the actual availability of water. Comparing these responses with administrative records would help identify differences between infrastructure reported as functional and services experienced by users.

Jal Jeevan Mission 2.0 offers an opportunity to improve the measurement of rural water supply. The challenge is to produce information that allows service reliability to be assessed consistently across districts and states.

## ONE PAPER WORTH READING

### Does community sanitation deprivation affect depression among older Indians?

Anoop Jain, Rockli Kim and S. V. Subramanian examine the association between community-level toilet deprivation and depressive symptoms among older adults in India in a paper published in *GeroScience* on 7 October 2026.

Their study uses data from the first wave of the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India, collected during 2017–2018. The analytical sample comprises 63,564 adults above the age of 45 living in 2,439 villages and urban wards.

The authors examine whether the proportion of residents living in households without toilets within a community is associated with depressive symptoms and probable major depression.

They estimate multilevel regression models that account for individual characteristics, household socioeconomic conditions, residential location and household toilet access.

The findings indicate a substantial association between community sanitation deprivation and mental health.

For one measure of depressive symptoms, respondents living in communities with the lowest levels of toilet coverage had an estimated odds ratio of 1.38, with a 95 per cent confidence interval of 1.08–1.76, relative to the model’s reference condition.

The authors report broadly similar patterns using alternative measures of probable major depression.

The analysis is interesting because community sanitation conditions may affect people through mechanisms extending beyond their own household facilities.

Living in an area with widespread sanitation deprivation may involve exposure to environmental contamination, difficulties accessing alternative facilities and social stigma associated with the residential environment.

The study examines whether community conditions modify the relationship between household toilet access and depression. Although descriptive patterns suggest higher depressive symptom prevalence in communities with lower toilet coverage, the estimated interaction terms were statistically insignificant.

There are important limitations.

The analysis is cross-sectional, and the data were collected in 2017–2018. Communities with poor sanitation may also experience other forms of deprivation associated with mental health. The regression adjustments cannot eliminate the possibility of unobserved confounding.

The study also lacks information about whether households without private toilets have access to shared or public facilities, which could influence the interpretation of sanitation deprivation.

These limitations restrict causal inference. The results nevertheless suggest that household-level sanitation indicators may provide an incomplete account of the environmental conditions associated with health.

I would recommend this paper to researchers working on sanitation, ageing and social determinants of health. Its principal contribution lies in specifying community-level sanitation deprivation as an exposure and examining its relationship with mental health outcomes using a large national dataset.

Future work using longitudinal observations could establish whether changes in community sanitation conditions precede changes in depressive symptoms. Qualitative research could also examine the proposed mechanisms involving stigma, environmental conditions and access to facilities.

## ONE NUMBER

### 41.9 per cent of women workers reported journeys exceeding one kilometre for work.

India’s National Household Travel Survey found that 41.9 per cent of female workers aged fifteen and above reported work-related journeys exceeding one kilometre. The corresponding proportion among male workers was 61.9 per cent.

The difference was twenty percentage points.

These estimates concern people classified as workers and measure participation in work-related travel above the survey’s distance threshold.

They cannot establish how much of the observed difference arises from transport access, employment arrangements, household responsibilities or other factors.

The findings nevertheless provide a useful basis for studying women’s geographical access to employment.

A more detailed analysis would examine employment type, commuting distance, earnings and the availability of public transport. It would also need to account for workers whose employment takes place at home or within one kilometre of their residence.

The national survey offers the possibility of investigating these relationships across rural and urban areas.

## EDITORIAL NOTE

Several developments in today’s edition concern the relationship between official statistics and the conditions people experience.

India’s environmental-economic accounting strategy seeks to improve information about natural resources and economic activity. The National Household Travel Survey introduces new measures of mobility, including differences between men and women that deserve closer analysis.

The announcements concerning banking, healthcare professionals and rural water supply show how administrative records can describe different parts of public service delivery.

Their policy value depends on the questions being asked of the data.

A register of healthcare professionals can inform workforce planning when qualifications and geographical distribution are recorded accurately. Reports of tap connections become more informative when accompanied by reliable measures of water availability.

The methodological questions concern definitions, coverage, accuracy and interpretation. Addressing them requires independent scrutiny of official statistics and the administrative systems from which they are produced.
