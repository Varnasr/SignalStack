## five things worth knowing

### 1. Nepal’s reconstruction estimate has jumped to NPR 723.31 billion

Nepal’s Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment estimates that recovery from the 26 August Bhotekoshi floods will require NPR 723.31 billion. Infrastructure accounts for NPR 473.57 billion, including NPR 390.62 billion for energy and electricity systems. Thirteen hydropower projects with 759 MW of capacity were affected, alongside 69 kilometres of roads and 33 motorable bridges that were washed away. ([The Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2026/09/10/rs723-billion-needed-to-rebuild-flood-hit-areas-government-assessment-finds?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The figures matter because they are more detailed than the preliminary estimates available earlier this week. They also separate immediate needs, NPR 8.73 billion over six months, from NPR 714.58 billion in longer-term reconstruction. ([The Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2026/09/10/rs723-billion-needed-to-rebuild-flood-hit-areas-government-assessment-finds?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Why it matters: the question now moves from estimating losses to deciding whether Nepal rebuilds the assets it lost or changes where and how infrastructure is built.

### 2. Nepal has made an early test case of the new loss-and-damage architecture

Nepal has sought an initial US$20 million from the Fund for Responding to Loss and Damage following the floods. The fund’s co-chairs have told Kathmandu that they are working with its board to consider Nepal’s request for urgent assistance. ([The Kathmandu Post](https://kathmandupost.com/national/2026/09/10/climate-loss-and-damage-fund-moves-to-consider-urgent-support-for-nepal?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The amount is small beside the NPR 723.31 billion recovery estimate. Its significance lies elsewhere. Loss-and-damage finance was created precisely for climate-vulnerable countries facing harms that adaptation cannot fully prevent, but converting that principle into rapid post-disaster finance remains largely untested.

Why it matters: Nepal could provide an early real-world test of how quickly the international loss-and-damage system can move from attribution and eligibility arguments to usable finance.

### 3. South-East Asia has adopted an unusually concrete medical-equipment policy

WHO South-East Asia Member States today adopted the region’s first dedicated resolution on medical devices, diagnostics and assistive technologies. Governments have committed to designate national lead mechanisms by 2027, complete baseline assessments by 2028 and develop or update costed national implementation plans by 2030. ([World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/southeastasia/news/detail/10-09-2026-who-south-east-asia-region-to-improve-access-to-medical-devices--in-vitro-diagnostics-and-assistive-technologies?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The interesting part is what countries will measure. The proposed monitoring system includes whether priority equipment is functional, preventive maintenance, downtime, consumable stock-outs and geographic inequalities in access. Procurement is also expected to account for installation, maintenance, training and total ownership costs. ([World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/southeastasia/news/detail/10-09-2026-who-south-east-asia-region-to-improve-access-to-medical-devices--in-vitro-diagnostics-and-assistive-technologies?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Why it matters: health systems routinely count machines purchased. This framework asks whether those machines can actually deliver a service.

### 4. Pakistan received US$3.66 billion in remittances in August

Workers’ remittances reached US$3.656 billion in August, 16.5 per cent above August 2025, according to State Bank of Pakistan data reported on 10 September. Inflows during July and August totalled US$7.3 billion, up 14.7 per cent year-on-year. ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/2028756/remittances-rise-165pc-in-august?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The increase needs interpretation. Pakistani businesspeople in Gulf states told *Dawn* that some of the rise may reflect investors liquidating assets and repatriating money amid regional uncertainty. If so, part of the recent increase is different from a sustained rise in migrant labour earnings. ([Dawn](https://www.dawn.com/news/2028756/remittances-rise-165pc-in-august?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Why it matters: remittances support household consumption and Pakistan’s external account, but identical balance-of-payments entries can arise from quite different household and investor behaviour.

### 5. India’s food-label fight has moved materially

India’s Food Safety and Standards Authority told the Supreme Court today that it is open to introducing stronger front-of-pack warning labels in a single phase. The proposed system would place a red warning symbol on products exceeding the threshold for any one of added sugar, salt or saturated fat, rather than requiring excess levels in two categories. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/india-food-regulator-says-it-is-open-stricter-labelling-after-court-scrutinizes-2026-09-10/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A second measurement dispute concerns the denominator. Food companies are pressing for thresholds based on serving sizes, while FSSAI’s proposal uses a standard 100 gram or 100 ml reference. ([The Economic Times](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/front-of-pack-labels-food-inc-bats-for-per-serving-benchmark-in-sc-not-fssais-per-100-gm-plan/articleshow/133981453.cms?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Why it matters: “per serving” sounds intuitive, but serving sizes can vary across products and manufacturers. The denominator can determine whether the same product receives a warning.

## one paper worth reading

### When inequality becomes political conflict

A new *World Development* paper asks whether changes in local economic inequality cause social unrest in rural India. The authors combine high-frequency bank-account information that tracks village-level inequality over time with data on unrest, and develop a model in which conflict emerges when local elites capture unequal shares of economic surplus. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305750X26001075?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Their identification strategy exploits rainfall-driven water shocks that disproportionately increase elite incomes. The headline estimate is substantial: a 10 per cent increase in their inequality measure raises the monthly probability of unrest by about 6.5 per cent. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305750X26001075?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The paper is unusually interesting because it moves below state and district averages and observes changing economic distributions within villages. The causal claim still depends on the exclusion restriction: rainfall-induced water shocks must affect unrest through the inequality mechanism being estimated rather than through other channels. In rural India, rainfall can alter employment, agricultural output, prices and local political relations, so that assumption deserves close attention.

The broader result matters. Distribution within a locality may affect political stability independently of average income. This is a paper I would flag for fuller treatment in a future flagship Research Rundown.

**Citation:** “Inequality and social unrest in India.” 2026. *World Development* 205: 107418. DOI: 10.1016/j.worlddev.2026.107418. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0305750X26001075?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## policy watch

### India’s food-label rules may become substantially stricter

The Supreme Court’s scrutiny of India’s proposed front-of-pack nutrition labelling regime produced a significant regulatory shift today. FSSAI indicated that it could abandon a phased approach and introduce stronger warning requirements immediately. ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/legal/litigation/india-food-regulator-says-it-is-open-stricter-labelling-after-court-scrutinizes-2026-09-10/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Two design choices now deserve close attention. The first is whether a product receives a warning after exceeding the threshold for one nutrient or only after exceeding several. The second is whether thresholds use a common per-100-gram or per-100-ml denominator or manufacturer-defined serving sizes. Industry representatives have challenged the former approach. ([The Economic Times](https://m.economictimes.com/industry/cons-products/food/front-of-pack-labels-food-inc-bats-for-per-serving-benchmark-in-sc-not-fssais-per-100-gm-plan/articleshow/133981453.cms?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

These look like technical details. They determine regulatory coverage.

A system based on variable serving sizes can make comparisons harder and create incentives around how a “serving” is defined. A standard denominator sacrifices some correspondence with actual consumption while giving consumers and regulators a common comparison unit. The final court and regulatory documents will therefore be worth reading closely.

## editorial note

### Reconstruction needs a counterfactual

Nepal’s NPR 723.31 billion estimate gives the disaster a price. It does not yet tell us the price of avoiding the next one.

Post-disaster assessments are good at counting damaged roads, power systems, houses and public facilities. The harder counterfactual concerns where those assets should have been located, what engineering standards would have reduced losses, and whether some investments should exist in the same form at all.

That distinction matters for climate finance. If “reconstruction” means replacing a washed-away bridge with a similar bridge in the same exposure zone, expenditure can restore capital without reducing vulnerability. If the replacement costs more because its location, design or redundancy changes, the additional expenditure is part of adaptation.

Nepal’s assessment gives researchers an unusually concrete case for examining this distinction. I would return to it once the full assessment documents and financing plan are available.

**Reader question:** What evidence should a post-disaster needs assessment require before classifying additional reconstruction expenditure as climate adaptation rather than ordinary asset replacement?
