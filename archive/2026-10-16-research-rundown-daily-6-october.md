*Research Rundown Daily 6 October 2026*

The World Bank released its latest South Asia economic outlook on 6 October, alongside country updates that reveal substantial differences in economic performance across the region. India also announced changes to its statistical system, while new administrative figures raised familiar questions about how public healthcare access should be measured.

## FIVE THINGS WORTH KNOWING

### 1. South Asia is expected to grow by 6.9 per cent, although the regional average conceals considerable differences.

The World Bank's October 2026 *South Asia Economic Update*, titled *Adopting AI for Growth*, projects regional economic growth of 6.9 per cent in 2026, followed by 6.7 per cent in 2027. Strong domestic demand, remittances and recent economic reforms have supported growth despite higher energy prices and uncertainty in international markets.

The report gives particular attention to artificial intelligence as a possible source of productivity growth. Approximately 23 per cent of Indian firms report using AI, compared with 43 per cent of firms in the United States. The Bank identifies opportunities in global value chains and public services, including agriculture, health and education.

The adoption figures require careful interpretation. Reported use tells us relatively little about the intensity of use, changes in productivity or consequences for employment. A firm that uses an AI application occasionally is quite different from one that has reorganised its production processes around the technology.

The immediate development question concerns the distribution of benefits. Smaller firms often lack the digital infrastructure, skilled workers and financing needed to adopt new technologies. Productivity gains concentrated among a relatively small number of larger firms would have different consequences for wages and employment from gains distributed across enterprises.

The report's emphasis on locally adapted applications is sensible, particularly where connectivity, language and administrative capacity remain constraints. Their effectiveness will need to be assessed through actual service outcomes and labour-market data.

[Source: World Bank, South Asia Economic Update, 6 October 2026](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/10/06/south-asia-s-strong-growth-defies-global-headwinds)

### 2. Bangladesh's slowdown is now visible in poverty, employment and banking indicators.

The World Bank's *Bangladesh Development Update*, released on 6 October, projects GDP growth of 3.4 per cent in both FY2026 and FY2027. This is substantially below the regional projection, although the fiscal-year and calendar-year estimates refer to different reporting periods.

The report identifies weaknesses in the banking sector, limited domestic revenue collection, energy constraints and sluggish private investment as continuing obstacles to recovery.

Its distributional findings deserve particular attention. The Bank estimates that approximately 2.1 million additional people were living in poverty in FY2026 compared with the preceding year. Employment creation has stalled, and women have experienced job losses.

Banking-sector distress has also worsened. The reported non-performing loan ratio increased from 30.6 per cent in December 2025 to 33.2 per cent in June 2026. Domestic revenue collection stood at approximately 8.3 per cent of GDP, leaving limited fiscal room for social expenditure and investment.

The social protection findings are particularly concerning. According to the report, approximately half of the poorest households remain outside social protection programmes. The government is developing a Dynamic Social Registry to improve beneficiary identification and enrolment.

Better targeting could reduce exclusion, although a registry cannot compensate for insufficient programme financing or weak delivery systems. Its performance should be judged by changes in coverage, exclusion errors, timeliness of transfers and the adequacy of benefits.

Bangladesh's current difficulties demonstrate how financial-sector problems, employment insecurity and limited fiscal capacity can reinforce one another. The consequences extend well beyond the national growth rate.

[Source: World Bank, Bangladesh Development Update, 6 October 2026](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/10/06/bangladesh-needs-urgent-financial-energy-and-revenue-reforms-to-restore-growth-cre)

### 3. Sri Lanka's economy has recovered its pre-crisis output, while poverty remains elevated.

Sri Lanka's economic recovery has reached an important milestone. According to the World Bank's *Sri Lanka Development Update*, released on 6 October, real GDP has returned to its 2018 level following twelve consecutive quarters of expansion.

The Bank projects growth of 4.4 per cent in 2026 and 4.2 per cent in 2027. Industry and services have contributed to the recovery, while fiscal performance has improved.

Household welfare has recovered more slowly. The report places poverty at 16.9 per cent, substantially above its pre-crisis level. Employment and household incomes continue to lag behind aggregate economic activity.

This distinction matters for assessing economic recovery. Returning to an earlier level of national output does not establish that households have regained their purchasing power or that employment opportunities have recovered across occupations and regions.

The report also identifies agribusiness as a possible source of future employment and investment. Primary agriculture accounts for approximately 8 per cent of GDP, while the wider agrifood system contributes around one-sixth of output and more than 40 per cent of employment.

Agricultural productivity therefore has consequences for a much larger workforce than the national accounts category for primary agriculture might suggest.

Sri Lanka's recovery needs to be assessed through household incomes, employment quality, real wages and poverty transitions alongside national output. These measures will establish whether economic stabilisation is translating into improvements in living standards.

[Source: World Bank, Sri Lanka Development Update, 6 October 2026](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2026/10/02/sri-lanka-s-economy-reaches-pre-crisis-levels-but-recovery-uneven)

### 4. India is trying to measure a larger share of its services economy every month.

The Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation released an approach paper on 6 October proposing to expand the coverage of India's Index of Services Production.

The experimental monthly index currently covers 19 broad service subsectors representing approximately 60 per cent of services-sector gross value added. It relies substantially on administrative records and information derived from GST transactions.

The proposed expansion would incorporate education, human health and residential care, and public administration and defence. Together, these activities represent approximately 21 per cent of services-sector GVA. The ministry estimates that the expanded index would cover approximately 78.4 per cent of services GVA.

This is an important statistical development because services account for more than half of India's gross value added, while high-frequency indicators have traditionally provided much better coverage of industrial production.

The methodological problems are substantial. Services delivered outside conventional market transactions present particular difficulties for measuring changes in real output. In education, for example, the number of students enrolled or classes delivered provides information about activity, while educational outcomes require separate measurement.

Health services present similar difficulties. Consultations, admissions and procedures measure different aspects of activity, and changes in their composition can complicate comparisons over time.

The usefulness of the proposed index will depend on the coverage of informal and non-market services, the construction of volume indicators and the treatment of changes in service quality.

MoSPI has invited comments on the approach paper until 16 October 2026. The consultation provides an opportunity for researchers working on national accounts, public services and administrative data to examine the proposed methods before they become part of regular statistical reporting.

[Source: Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation, 6 October 2026](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2319661&lang=1&reg=3)

### 5. India's eSanjeevani platform has crossed 500 million consultations. The next question concerns continuity of care.

The Ministry of Health and Family Welfare announced on 6 October that eSanjeevani, India's national telemedicine service, had recorded more than 50 crore consultations.

The cumulative figure reached 500,092,730 on 15 September 2026. According to the ministry, women accounted for 57.14 per cent of utilisation, while people aged 46 years and above accounted for 40.15 per cent.

These figures indicate considerable use of digital consultations within the public healthcare system. They also raise questions about what administrative utilisation statistics can establish.

The number of consultations is different from the number of patients served. Repeat consultations may reflect appropriate follow-up care, continuing health needs or difficulties obtaining treatment elsewhere.

A fuller assessment would examine the proportion of consultations resulting in completed treatment, referrals requiring further care, medicine availability, continuity of care and patient outcomes.

There is also a geographical question. National totals may conceal differences in the availability of telemedicine services across districts and population groups. The distribution of consultations across socioeconomic categories would be especially useful for assessing equity.

The reported scale of eSanjeevani makes these questions worth examining systematically. Administrative data can provide a starting point, although claims about improvements in health outcomes require additional evidence.

## ONE PAPER WORTH READING

### Does migration increase the likelihood of experiencing discrimination in later life?

Vasim Ahamad, Rahul Rahul, Ram B. Bhagat and T. V. Sekher examine the association between internal migration and perceived discrimination among Indians aged 45 and above in a paper published in *BMC Public Health* on 5 October 2026.

The authors analyse 64,711 respondents from the first wave of the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India, conducted during 2017–2018. They classify migration using respondents' place of last residence and measure perceived discrimination through an everyday discrimination scale.

Approximately 57 per cent of respondents were classified as internal migrants. After adjusting for observed socioeconomic characteristics, migrants had 1.38 times the odds of reporting discrimination compared with non-migrants, with a 95 per cent confidence interval of 1.28–1.48. The association was stronger among men, interstate migrants and recent migrants.

The paper offers useful evidence on an understudied dimension of ageing in India. Its principal limitation concerns causal interpretation. Cross-sectional associations cannot establish whether migration caused discrimination, and the migrant category captures movement over the life course rather than a single type of migration experience. The subjective discrimination measure also requires careful interpretation.

The findings justify closer examination of migration histories, gender, social identity and access to services among older adults. Longitudinal analysis would help establish how these relationships change with duration of residence.

## ONE NUMBER

### 33.2 per cent

This was Bangladesh's reported non-performing loan ratio in June 2026, according to the World Bank's latest development update. The corresponding figure in December 2025 was 30.6 per cent.

The increase matters because bank balance-sheet problems can restrict credit, discourage investment and impose additional demands on financial supervision and public resources.

The ratio measures the share of loans classified as non-performing. It does not directly measure losses ultimately incurred by banks, which also depend on recovery, provisioning and collateral values.

For Bangladesh, changes in this indicator should be read alongside trends in private credit, investment and employment.

## POLICY WATCH

### NAVYA 2.0 will expand vocational training to 100 Aspirational Districts.

The Ministry of Skill Development and Entrepreneurship and the Ministry of Women and Child Development held a preparatory orientation for NAVYA 2.0 on 6 October.

The programme targets young women and adolescent girls aged 16–25 and is intended to operate across 100 Aspirational Districts in 24 states and Union Territories. Its predecessor was implemented in 27 districts across 19 states and Union Territories.

The ministries also released standard operating procedures for the expanded programme.

Its stated objective is to connect vocational training with apprenticeships, entrepreneurship and livelihood opportunities. The important distinction is between participation in a training programme and successful entry into paid work.

Programme monitoring should therefore record completion, placement, earnings, job retention and changes in employment status. These outcomes should be disaggregated by age, educational attainment, social group and location.

The earlier pilot also offers an opportunity for learning. Evidence on which training courses produced sustained employment, which participants completed them and why others dropped out would be useful before expansion.

The 6 October announcement concerns preparations for implementation. Actual coverage and results will have to be assessed as the programme is rolled out.

## EDITORIAL NOTE

The economic reports released today provide a useful illustration of why growth statistics need to be read alongside distributional indicators. South Asia's projected growth rate is high, while Bangladesh faces worsening poverty and Sri Lanka continues to struggle with the household consequences of its economic crisis.

India's announcements present a related measurement problem. A larger services index could improve the timeliness of economic statistics, and eSanjeevani's administrative records provide evidence of substantial service use. Their value for policy will depend on what they capture, whom they exclude and how they relate to outcomes.

Better measurement begins with identifying the question each indicator can answer.
