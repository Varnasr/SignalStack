# Good Reads #15 | Who owns intelligence? Arundhati Roy on AI and extraction

*2026-10-17. Published at [https://varna.substack.com/p/good-reads-15-who-owns-intelligence](https://varna.substack.com/p/good-reads-15-who-owns-intelligence). This file is a generated copy; the Substack post is the original.*

Arundhati Roy begins her new essay with a small irritation that many writers will recognise. Artificial intelligence has made even the ordinary habits of writing suspect. A familiar turn of phrase, an unusual sentence or a particular punctuation mark may now be interpreted as evidence that a machine produced the text. For a novelist who has spent decades developing a distinctive voice, this is an unsettling prospect.

By the end of *Apocalypse Now and Then*, published in EQUATOR on 15 October, Roy has moved considerably further. She believes artificial intelligence threatens the relationship between human beings and language, transfers the accumulated knowledge of civilisation into corporate hands, and may eventually make human labour and thought dispensable.

It is an angry essay, filled with historical comparisons and unsettling predictions. Roy writes as a novelist and political critic, and she makes no effort to conceal her convictions. Some of her claims are speculative. Others concern economic and political arrangements that can already be examined through available evidence.

I found the latter considerably more persuasive.

## Who gets to own what we know?

The most compelling part of Roy's essay concerns her own experience with Anthropic. She describes receiving a notice offering compensation for the use of her books in training a large language model. The books had reportedly been obtained from websites distributing unauthorised copies.

The settlement is substantial. On 20 July 2026, a federal court in California granted final approval to the class-action settlement in *Bartz v. Anthropic*, concerning books obtained from unauthorised digital libraries. The litigation has also raised wider questions about the legal treatment of copyrighted works in AI training.

Roy's response is personal, although the problem extends far beyond publishing. She has spent years producing books whose language, arguments and literary form belong to her work as a writer. A technology company has incorporated copies of that work into a commercial process that she neither initiated nor authorised.

Copyright law provides one framework for addressing the dispute. The economic problem is considerably broader.

Large AI systems depend on enormous quantities of material produced by writers, artists, software developers, scientists, researchers and public institutions. Some of that material is copyrighted, some is licensed for particular uses, and some belongs to the public domain. The legal conditions differ. So do the circumstances under which the material was originally produced.

Research universities, for example, generate knowledge through a combination of public financing, academic labour, private funding and institutional resources. Their publications may circulate through commercial journals, open repositories and databases. AI companies can subsequently draw on these accumulated bodies of knowledge to develop systems sold to universities, governments and businesses.

The result raises questions about the distribution of economic returns across institutions. Public expenditure may contribute to the creation of knowledge while commercial firms control the infrastructure through which it is subsequently processed and sold.

The distinction between owning information and controlling access to services built around it deserves closer examination. AI firms are acquiring positions within the infrastructure of knowledge production. Their pricing, licensing arrangements and technical restrictions can influence who can use those services and on what terms.

This gives Roy's argument a wider significance. Compensation for authors addresses one particular form of appropriation. Publicly financed knowledge, academic research and collective intellectual work present additional questions for which existing copyright arrangements provide only partial answers.

Roy pushes the claim much further, suggesting that corporations may eventually control access to human knowledge itself. That outcome remains speculative. The existing concentration of computing capacity, proprietary models and commercial distribution systems is sufficient reason to examine the problem.

## The corporation and the state

Roy compares the expansion of AI corporations with the history of the East India Company. India's colonial experience makes the comparison particularly forceful. A commercial enterprise acquired political authority, exercised control over territory and resources, and became part of the machinery of imperial rule.

Contemporary technology corporations operate under very different legal and political conditions. Their relationship with governments nevertheless deserves scrutiny.

AI companies provide services to public institutions and develop systems used in administration, surveillance, defence and other government activities. Governments, in turn, influence the industry's development through procurement contracts, infrastructure investment, regulation and access to public resources.

Roy is especially suspicious of technology executives who warn about existential risks while asking governments to regulate the industry. She suspects that large corporations may benefit from regulation that establishes their legitimacy while raising the costs faced by smaller competitors.

There is a familiar political economy problem here. Firms with considerable financial and technical resources can influence the rules governing their industries. Compliance requirements may impose proportionately greater costs on new entrants. Public authorities can also become dependent on the companies whose conduct they are expected to oversee.

The argument needs greater discrimination than Roy allows. Regulatory proposals differ in their objectives, obligations and enforcement arrangements. Competition law, data protection, procurement rules and independent technical audits can all restrict the discretion of established firms. Their effects depend on institutional design and implementation.

The more immediate concern is the extent to which states retain the capacity to make independent decisions about systems on which essential public functions depend.

For India, this includes the terms governing government contracts for cloud services, access to public datasets, the location and ownership of computing infrastructure, and the possibility of transferring services between providers.

The concentration of technological capacity creates problems for public accountability when governments lack the personnel, information or alternative suppliers necessary to scrutinise procurement decisions.

Roy's historical analogy makes these relationships visible, although the comparison cannot establish that contemporary AI companies will acquire powers equivalent to those exercised by colonial enterprises.

The present distribution of commercial and public authority deserves investigation in its own right.

## The workers and resources behind the machine

Roy is particularly effective when she turns to the physical economy of artificial intelligence. Computers require electricity, data centres require land and cooling systems, and AI development depends on workers performing tasks distributed across several countries.

These systems have measurable costs.

The International Energy Agency estimated that data centres consumed approximately 415 terawatt-hours of electricity worldwide in 2024, representing about 1.5 per cent of global electricity consumption. Its 2025 *Energy and AI* report projected consumption of approximately 945 terawatt-hours by 2030 under its base-case scenario.

The projection covers data centres generally, with AI accounting for a substantial part of the expected increase. Its uncertainty is considerable because future electricity demand depends on the pace of adoption, improvements in computing efficiency and constraints within energy systems.

The national figures are useful, although the consequences of new facilities are often local. Data centres may create concentrated demands for electricity and water in places where infrastructure already serves competing users.

Roy approaches this problem through an account of a rayon factory established along the Chaliyar River in Kerala during the state's early period of industrial development. The factory created industrial employment while, in her account, its operations damaged the river and affected people whose livelihoods depended on the surrounding environment.

The historical details merit independent examination. The distributional problem she identifies is familiar to development economists.

The financial accounts of an industrial enterprise may record the value of its output, investment and direct employment. Its activities can also impose environmental costs and affect employment in other sectors. Those costs may be borne by households and communities with limited influence over the original investment decision.

The comparison is relevant to India's emerging data-centre industry. An adequate assessment would consider the employment created during construction and operation, the value of investment, the public concessions offered, and the demands placed on local infrastructure.

Environmental assessment is particularly important where electricity and water supplies are constrained. The opportunity cost of allocating these resources to one activity depends on what other activities might have used them.

Roy also draws attention to workers engaged in data collection, annotation and related activities. Some of this work takes place through subcontracting arrangements that make employment conditions and responsibility for labour protections difficult to establish.

She anticipates that workers who help develop AI systems may eventually find some of their own tasks automated. The possibility deserves investigation, although her predictions about widespread displacement exceed what can currently be established.

The International Labour Organization's 2025 assessment estimated that one-quarter of global employment was in occupations with some exposure to generative AI. Approximately 3.3 per cent fell into the highest exposure category.

Occupational exposure measures the potential for AI systems to perform particular tasks. Actual employment consequences depend on the decisions of employers, the costs of adoption, changes in demand and the capacity of workers to move into other activities.

Even where technology increases productivity, the allocation of the resulting gains remains a matter of economic institutions and bargaining power.

Roy's concerns about subcontracted labour are therefore worth investigating independently of her predictions about the eventual disappearance of work.

## Where Roy's argument becomes less convincing

The essay makes several claims about the future of human intelligence and independent thought. Roy worries that reliance on AI-generated language will weaken people's capacity to reason, create and recognise authentic expression.

Her concerns are understandable, particularly in education and academic writing. The widespread availability of generated text creates new problems for assessment, authorship and the evaluation of intellectual work.

The evidence concerning long-term cognitive effects remains incomplete. Existing research examines different uses of AI under different conditions. It cannot yet establish that dependence on these systems will produce widespread or irreversible cognitive decline.

The distinction between different forms of use is significant. A student who delegates an entire assignment to a machine may learn very little from the exercise. A researcher who uses software to compare alternative specifications while independently checking the assumptions and results is engaged in a different activity.

The outcomes depend partly on the organisation of work and the demands placed on users.

Roy's argument also gives limited attention to the potential benefits of AI applications in settings where professional services are scarce. Translation, accessibility tools and selected administrative applications may reduce the time or cost required to perform certain tasks.

Claims about these benefits also require empirical testing. Evidence of faster task completion, for example, does not establish improvements in accuracy, equity or final outcomes. The appropriate assessment depends on the particular application.

Roy's larger claim that artificial intelligence represents capitalism's final destination is difficult to evaluate as an empirical proposition. It expresses a political judgment about the concentration of ownership and the extraction of value from human activity.

The judgment draws attention to existing arrangements. Its predictive force is less clear.

## The question that remains

I came away from Roy's essay thinking about the terms on which societies are allowing AI infrastructure to develop.

Consider a public university that pays commercial providers for access to AI services trained partly on publicly financed research. Or a government that offers infrastructure concessions to attract data-centre investment without publishing a complete assessment of the environmental costs. Or workers who perform essential tasks in AI supply chains while remaining outside the employment protections available to directly hired staff.

These examples concern arrangements that can be examined through contracts, expenditure records, labour surveys and environmental assessments. The distribution of costs and benefits is open to empirical investigation.

Roy is more interested in the larger political question of who should control the technology. She is sceptical of proposals that compensate the public financially while leaving corporate ownership of the underlying systems intact.

There is a substantial argument here, although the alternatives also require examination. Public ownership can create new problems of accountability. Regulation can fail through weak enforcement or the influence of established firms. National control can leave existing inequalities within a country undisturbed.

The quality of the institutions governing technology will therefore influence its distributional consequences.

I would recommend *Apocalypse Now and Then* for the questions Roy raises about knowledge, ownership and political power. The essay is uneven. Its predictions are sometimes far more confident than the evidence permits, and its historical comparisons need greater care. Her account of the material resources and human work behind AI provides a useful corrective to discussions that treat these systems as autonomous technological achievements.

For researchers in development economics, the most productive response is to examine the arrangements through which public resources, labour and knowledge contribute to privately controlled systems, and to establish who receives the returns.

Roy believes those arrangements are already producing a dangerous concentration of power. There is enough evidence of concentration to justify serious public scrutiny, even while its eventual consequences remain uncertain.
